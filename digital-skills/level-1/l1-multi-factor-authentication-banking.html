Three Non-Negotiable Exam Golden Rules

Golden Rule 1: True MFA Combines 2+ DIFFERENT Factor Categories

Authentication falls into: (1) Something You KNOW (Password/PIN), (2) Something You HAVE (Phone App/USB Security Key), (3) Something You ARE (Fingerprint/Face ID).

✗ Entering a Password PLUS a Secret Question (both are Knowledge — NOT true MFA!)
✓ Strong Password (Know) + Authenticator App 6-digit TOTP / Biometric (Have/Are)
Golden Rule 2: Prefer Authenticator Apps Over SMS Text Codes Where Available

Authenticator apps (Microsoft/Google Authenticator) stay tied cleanly to encrypted hardware and resist SIM-swap interception attacks far better than plain SMS.

✗ Using easily intercepted SMS only when hardware key or Authenticator app is supported
✓ Use Authenticator App push/TOTP codes + never click Approve on unexpected logins
Golden Rule 3: NEVER Share One-Time Passcodes (OTPs) Over Phone Calls!

Scammers posing as bank fraud teams ask victims to read back the 6-digit SMS code to 'cancel a payment' — that code actually authorises THEIR login/transfer!

✗ Reading an OTP code aloud to someone claiming to be 'Bank Fraud Team'
✓ Hang up immediately and dial 159 (official UK Stop Scams banking line) on another phone if possible

Step-by-Step Teaching & Key Concepts

1
Why Passwords Alone Are No Longer Enough

Billions of usernames and passwords are leaked on the dark web every year. If you use the same password on multiple websites, a data breach at one company puts all your accounts at risk.

Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA / 2FA) adds an extra layer of security. Even if a criminal steals your password, they CANNOT log in without your second authentication factor!

2
The Three Factors of Authentication

MFA combines at least two different categories:

  1. Something you KNOW: Password, PIN, or memorable security question.
  2. Something you HAVE: Physical smartphone receiving an SMS code, authenticator app (e.g. Microsoft Authenticator), or a physical hardware security key (YubiKey).
  3. Something you ARE (Biometrics): Fingerprint scan, facial recognition (Face ID), or retina scan.
Two-Factor Verification Prompt

3
Online Banking Security Rules

When managing business or personal finances online:

  • Always log out completely when finished.
  • Check statements weekly for unauthorised micro-charges.
  • Never approve an MFA prompt on your phone unless you are actively logging in yourself! Criminals who steal passwords trigger MFA prompts hoping you will click 'Approve' without thinking (MFA fatigue attack).

Worked Model 1 • Core StandardStep-by-Step Accredited Guide
Exam Scenario Prompt: A dental practice introduces Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA) for its staff patient database. Staff must now type their password AND enter a 6-digit code sent to their mobile phone. Explain how this prevents unauthorised access if a worker's password is stolen in a phishing attack.
Step 1: Explain the protection mechanism [B1]
The criminal only has the password (something you know), but they do not possess the worker's physical mobile phone (something you have) to receive the 6-digit code.
Tutor Marking Rationale: Identifies the requirement for two independent factors.
Step 2: State outcome [B1]
Without the second factor, the login attempt is blocked and patient records remain protected.
Tutor Marking Rationale: Confirms system denies unauthorized access.
Tutor Abdul's Exam Golden Rule: In Level 1 exams, questions on MFA test whether you know the three factors: something you know (password), something you have (phone/token), something you are (fingerprint).
Worked Model 2 • Applied Workplace Scenario Exam Scenario Model 2 • Responding Safely to Unexpected MFA Login Prompt ('MFA Fatigue')
Exam Scenario Prompt 2: While watching TV at 10 PM, a clinic manager's phone suddenly receives three Authenticator 'Approve Sign-In Request?' pop-ups she did NOT initiate. State what has happened and her exact two immediate actions.
Step 1: Diagnose Leaked Password & Reject Prompt Immediately [B1][M1]
Tap 'DENY / Report Fraudulent Request' immediately — someone already holds her password and is trying to push through MFA ('MFA fatigue'). Never tap Approve to silence notifications!
Tutor Marking Rationale: Recognises exact symptom of stolen first-factor credentials.
Step 2: Change Password Everywhere It Was Used & Alert IT [B1][M1]
Sign in cleanly directly, change her account password to a strong unique 3-random-word passphrase (and change any other service sharing the old password), then notify IT/Security.
Tutor Marking Rationale: Stops further unauthorised probes permanently.
Tutor Abdul's Distinction Tip: Remember: typing a PIN AND your password isn't 2-Factor Authentication — both belong to 'Something You Know'. Two distinct categories make MFA effective!

Critical Examiner Traps & Common Pitfalls

Trap 1: Thinking Two Knowledge Checks Count as Two-Factor (2FA)
Examiners regularly test whether `Password + Memorable Word` equals MFA — it does NOT!
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Confirm at least two DIFFERENT factor categories (e.g. Know + Have, or Know + Are).
Trap 2: Clicking 'Trust This Device Forever' on Shared Work/Library PCs
Allows the next person using that browser to bypass MFA challenges for 30 days.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Only remember trusted private personal hardware — never public or shared devices.
Trap 3: Losing Recovery Backup Codes After Upgrading Phones
Wiping an old phone before migrating Authenticator tokens locks you out cleanly.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Print/store encrypted offline MFA backup recovery codes safely before switching handsets.

Accredited Exam Benchmark Solution

Definition: Multi-Factor Authentication is a security process requiring users to provide two or more different forms of identification before gaining access. Factor 1 (Something you know): A strong private password or secret PIN. Factor 2 (Something you have/are): A one-time passcode generated on a registered authenticator app / phone, or a biometric fingerprint scan.
Official Marking Criteria:
  • [1 Mark]: Accurate definition of MFA requiring 2+ distinct verification steps.
  • [1 Mark]: Providing valid examples of two different authentication categories.
