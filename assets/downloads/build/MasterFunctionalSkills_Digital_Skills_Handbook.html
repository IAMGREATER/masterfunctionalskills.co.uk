Hardware, OS & Cloud Architecture

Modern workplace computing: high-performance hardware, multi-screen workstations, and cloud sync.
▶Essential Computing Infrastructure • Plain English Core Guide
Operating System (OS): The master software running your computer or phone (Windows, macOS, ChromeOS, Android). It manages your hardware, runs programs like Word or browsers, and provides accessibility settings (screen zoom, high contrast).
File Hierarchy & Folders: Organising work into nested folders and subfolders (like dividers in a filing cabinet). Standard file extensions (.docx, .xlsx, .pdf, .jpg) tell your device which program opens the file.
Cloud Storage & Syncing: Storing files on secure internet servers (OneDrive, Google Drive, iCloud) so files automatically update across all your devices and are safe from theft or hardware failure.
Input & Output Hardware: Input devices (keyboard, mouse, webcam, microphone) send information into the computer; output devices (monitors, printers, speakers) present information to you.
Network & Wi-Fi Security: Connecting to the internet safely using password-protected private Wi-Fi (WPA2/WPA3). Never do online banking or enter credentials on unencrypted public Wi-Fi.
Accessibility Tools: Built-in features like screen magnifiers, text-to-speech readers, speech-to-text dictation, and dyslexia color filters that make computers usable for everyone.
✓Security Golden Rule
Always check the file extension before opening email attachments (.exe, .bat, or .scr often signify dangerous malware).
!Workplace Trap!
Remember to lock your workstation screen (Win + L) whenever you step away from your desk to protect confidential data.

Unit 1 • Operating Systems, File Hierarchy & Cloud Storage

Domain 1: Structured folder hierarchies, file naming conventions, and cloud storage.
▶Core Knowledge & Specialist Terms Explained

Operating System (OS): The operating system is the main master program that makes your computer, tablet, or smartphone work. Examples include Windows on PCs, macOS on Apple Macs, ChromeOS on Chromebooks, and Android or iOS on mobile phones. What it does: The OS is like the manager of your device. It lets your screen, keyboard, and mouse communicate with your software (like Word or web browsers), and lets you adjust essential settings such as screen brightness, volume, Wi-Fi connections, and accessibility tools (like screen zoom and high contrast).

File Hierarchy & Folders: A file hierarchy means organising your computer files into main folders and subfolders, just like placing paper documents into labelled dividers inside a filing cabinet drawer. Example: Instead of dumping all your files on the Desktop, create a main folder called Documents, a subfolder called Customers, and inside that a folder called Invoices. This stops your computer getting cluttered and helps you find documents in seconds.

File Extensions & Naming: A file extension is the short code after the dot at the end of a file name that tells the computer what type of file it is. Common extensions include: .docx for Word documents, .xlsx for Excel spreadsheets, .pdf for read-only documents that look identical on all screens, and .jpg or .png for photos. Always use clear file names with dates and version numbers (e.g. 2026-10-25_Staff_Rota_v2.docx).

Cloud Storage & Syncing: Cloud storage means saving your files on secure internet servers (such as Microsoft OneDrive, Google Drive, or iCloud) rather than only on your physical computer hard drive. Synchronization (syncing) automatically updates your files across all your devices. Example: If you start an assignment at college or work, it syncs to the cloud so you can open and edit the exact same document at home on your tablet or phone. Cloud storage also protects your work if your laptop is lost, damaged, or stolen.

Unit 2 • Advanced Search Syntax & Information Verification

Domain 1: Advanced search operators (AND, OR, quotes), source evaluation, and fact-checking.
▶Core Knowledge & Specialist Terms Explained

Search Engines & Search Operators: When you search for information online (using Google, Bing, or DuckDuckGo), typing whole conversational sentences often brings up millions of irrelevant results or advertisements. Search operators are special words and symbols you type into the search box to filter your results with precision:

  • Quotation Marks ("..."): Searches for the exact words together in that precise order. Example: "national minimum wage rates" finds only pages with that exact phrase.
  • Minus Sign (-): Removes unwanted words from your search. Example: jaguar speed -car finds how fast the wild animal runs and filters out car adverts.
  • Site Search (site:): Limits results to one specific website or domain type. Example: apprenticeship funding site:.gov.uk searches only official UK government web pages.
  • Filetype (filetype:): Finds specific file formats. Example: health and safety policy filetype:pdf searches only downloadable PDF guides.

The CRAAP Test (Evaluating Sources): Not everything on the internet is accurate, truthful, or up to date. In education and employment, you must check sources using the 5-step CRAAP test:

  • C • Currency: When was the page published or updated? (Is the information still current, or has the law changed?)
  • R • Relevance: Does the information directly answer your workplace question?
  • A • Authority: Who wrote it? Is the author an expert, an official organisation (like GOV.UK or the NHS), or an unverified social media account?
  • A • Accuracy: Are the facts supported by evidence, official data, or links to primary sources?
  • P • Purpose: Why does this page exist? Is it to inform and educate you, or is it trying to sell you a product, collect your data, or push a biased opinion?

Unit 3 • Professional Word Processing & Structured Documents

Domain 2: Typographic hierarchy, styles, table formatting, and professional document proofreading.
▶Core Knowledge & Specialist Terms Explained

Word Processing Basics: Applications like Microsoft Word and Google Docs are used to create letters, reports, memos, and leaflets. To look professional, your documents need a clear layout, consistent fonts, and tidy margins.

Paragraph Styles (Heading 1, Heading 2 & Normal): Rather than highlighting titles and manually making them bigger or bold, you should click the pre-set Styles on the toolbar ribbon. Definition: Styles are pre-set text formatting rules that keep titles, subtitles, and paragraphs looking uniform throughout your whole document.
Three Major Benefits:
1. Consistency: If you change font themes, every title in your 20-page document updates in one click.
2. Accessibility: Screen-reading software used by visually impaired colleagues uses heading styles (H1, H2) to help them navigate between chapters.
3. Automatic Table of Contents: Word uses your Heading 1 and Heading 2 styles to create an automated table of contents with correct page numbers instantly!

Page Layout, Spacing & Page Breaks:
• Page Breaks (Ctrl + Enter): A command that neatly starts a new page. Golden Rule: Never press 'Enter' 20 times to reach the next page! If you add or delete words later, all your paragraphs will shift out of position.
• Line Spacing & Alignment: Keep body text left-aligned with 1.15 or 1.5 line spacing. Avoid 'justified' alignment, which creates irregular white spaces between words that are difficult for people with dyslexia to read.
• Alt-Text (Alternative Text): A short written description added to a photo or chart (e.g. right-click image → Edit Alt-Text). A screen reader reads this aloud to visually impaired readers so they know what the image shows.

Unit 4 • Spreadsheet Modeling, Formulas & Functions

Domain 2: Dynamic formula modeling (=SUM, =AVERAGE), absolute cell references, and grid formatting.
▶Core Knowledge & Specialist Terms Explained

Spreadsheets Explained: A spreadsheet (such as Microsoft Excel or Google Sheets) is a software grid made of vertical columns (labelled with letters A, B, C...) and horizontal rows (labelled with numbers 1, 2, 3...). Each box is called a cell. A cell is identified by its cell reference (its coordinates), such as B4 (Column B, Row 4).

Formulas vs Functions:
• Formulas: Calculations you create yourself. Every formula MUST begin with an equals sign (=). Use arithmetic symbols: + (add), - (subtract), * (multiply), and / (divide). Example: =B2*C2 multiplies the quantity in cell B2 by the unit price in cell C2.
• Functions: Built-in mathematical shortcuts that perform calculations on a whole group (range) of cells:
  • =SUM(D2:D20) → Adds together all numbers in the range D2 to D20.
  • =AVERAGE(D2:D20) → Calculates the mean (average) value.
  • =MIN(D2:D20) → Finds the lowest value in the range.
  • =MAX(D2:D20) → Finds the highest value in the range.
  • =COUNT(D2:D20) → Counts how many cells contain numbers.
  • =IF(C2<10, "Reorder", "OK") → Checks a condition: if stock in C2 is less than 10, it writes 'Reorder', otherwise 'OK'.

Relative vs Absolute Referencing:
• Relative Reference (e.g. B2): When you drag the formula down to the next row, it automatically changes to B3, B4, etc. This is perfect for row-by-row totals.
• Absolute Reference (e.g. $B$1): The dollar signs lock the cell coordinate in place. When you drag the formula down, it ALWAYS refers back to cell B1. Example: Storing a 20% VAT rate or £0.45 mileage rate in cell B1 and locking it as $B$1 so every calculation uses that exact rate.

Formatting Cells: Always format numbers using the toolbar (e.g. click Currency (£) to show two decimal places). Warning: Never manually type a pound sign '£' into a calculation cell, or the spreadsheet will treat it as text and produce an error (like #VALUE!)!

Unit 6 • Professional Email Etiquette, CC/BCC & Data Privacy

Domain 3: Professional subject lines, secure attachments, and data privacy protocols (CC vs BCC).
▶Core Knowledge & Specialist Terms Explained

Workplace Email Protocols: Email is the primary written communication tool in employment. Unlike a quick text or messaging app, a workplace email is a permanent, formal record that can be used as evidence in business disputes. Always maintain a polite, respectful tone, use proper spelling and grammar, and avoid slang or emojis.

Understanding Address Fields (To, CC, and BCC):
• To: The main person (or people) who the email is addressed to, and who is expected to reply or take action.
• CC (Carbon Copy): People who need to see the message for information, but who do not need to reply. Example: Sending a completed report to a client and putting your manager in CC so they know it was delivered.
• BCC (Blind Carbon Copy): Sends a copy of the email while completely hiding the recipient's email address from everyone else on the list.
Crucial Data Protection Rule (UK GDPR): Whenever you email a group of customers, clients, students, or patients, you must put their addresses in BCC. If you put them in 'To' or 'CC', everyone can see each other's private email addresses. Exposing customer contact details without consent is a serious legal data breach that can result in heavy fines!

Email Structure & Netiquette:
• Subject Line: Always write a clear, helpful subject line that summarizes the topic (e.g. Urgent: Staff Meeting Rescheduled to Thursday 14:00 instead of just Meeting or leaving it empty).
• Salutation & Sign-off: Open with a polite greeting (Dear Mr Davis, or Good morning Sarah,) and close with a professional sign-off (Kind regards, followed by your name and job title).
• No ALL CAPS: Never write emails in ALL CAPITAL LETTERS—in digital communication, this is seen as shouting and is considered aggressive.
• Attachments: Keep file attachments under 10 MB so they do not bounce back or fill up the recipient's mailbox. For larger files, send a secure cloud sharing link instead.

Checking for a Secure Connection (HTTPS & The SSL Padlock): Before you enter any personal details, passwords, or bank card numbers on a shopping website, always look at the browser address bar:

• Strong Customer Authentication (3D Secure): When you buy something online, your bank will often ask you to approve the purchase by entering a one-time code sent to your phone or confirming it in your banking app. This stops criminals from using your card if it is stolen.
• Order Verification: Always review your digital shopping basket before paying (checking quantities, postage fees, and VAT). Save the confirmation page and download the PDF receipt with the order reference number in case you need a refund.

▶Core Knowledge & Specialist Terms Explained

Staying Safe Online: Most cybercrime succeeds by tricking people into making mistakes rather than hacking code. Staying safe requires vigilant personal habits and knowing key security rules.

Phishing (Spotting Fraudulent Messages):
Definition: Phishing is when criminals send fake emails or texts (smishing) impersonating trusted organisations (banks, Royal Mail, Netflix, HMRC) to steal passwords, card details, or download malware.
Warning Signs:
1. Artificial Urgency: Demanding immediate action under threat of account suspension or fines.
2. Suspicious Sender: Display name looks real, but actual email address is unfamiliar.
3. Generic Greetings: 'Dear Customer' instead of your name.
4. Requests for PINs: Banks and HMRC never ask for passwords or PINs by email or text.
5. Suspicious Links: Always hover over links to check the real URL before clicking.

Strong Passwords & Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA):
• Three Random Words: The NCSC recommends passphrases of three random, memorable words with numbers/symbols (e.g. Purple$Pebble72Blanket!)—virtually impossible to crack, yet easy to remember.
• Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA): Requires your password plus a temporary code sent to your phone or app. Criminals cannot log in without your physical device.

UK GDPR & Personal Data Rights:
Definition: The UK GDPR protects living individuals' personal data (names, addresses, health notes, financial records).

Digital Skills & Cybersecurity Glossary (Part 1)

Absolute Cell Reference ($A$1)
A locked cell coordinate in a spreadsheet formula using dollar signs ($). It stays fixed on that exact cell when autofilled down a column.
Alt-Text (Alternative Text)
A short written description added to an image or chart. A screen reader reads this aloud to visually impaired readers so they know what the picture displays.
Cloud Storage & Syncing
Saving your files on secure internet servers (like OneDrive, Google Drive, or iCloud). Files update (sync) automatically across all your devices and are safe from loss.
Cybersecurity Hygiene
Everyday security habits that protect your data, such as using strong passphrases, never sharing logins, locking your screen, and running software updates.
Data Protection / UK GDPR
UK law that protects individuals' personal data (names, medical notes, bank details). Organisations must keep data confidential and report breaches within 72 hours.
DSE & Ergonomics
Display Screen Equipment health rules for setting up your desk, chair, and monitor to avoid back, neck, and eye strain, including the 20-20-20 visual rest rule.

Digital Skills & Cybersecurity Glossary (Part 2)

E-Commerce
Buying, selling, or paying for goods and services over the internet, such as online shopping, booking tickets, or paying bills through secure payment systems.
File Hierarchy & Extensions
Organising files into labelled folders and subfolders. File extensions (.docx, .xlsx, .pdf, .jpg) are codes after the dot that tell computers which software opens the file.
Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)
A security system that requires two steps to log in: your password PLUS a temporary code sent to your phone or app. Keeps accounts safe even if passwords leak.
Netiquette
Polite and respectful rules of digital communication, such as using clear email subject lines, avoiding ALL CAPS (which feels like shouting), and muting on video calls.

Digital Skills & Cybersecurity Glossary (Part 3)

Phishing
A fake email or text pretending to be from a trusted company (like your bank or Royal Mail) to trick you into clicking a link, downloading a virus, or giving away passwords.
SSL / HTTPS & Padlock
A locked padlock symbol and 'https://' in your browser showing that your connection is encrypted, scrambling your card numbers and passwords so thieves cannot steal them.
Subject Access Request (SAR)
A legal right under UK GDPR allowing any individual to request a free copy of all personal records and data an organisation holds about them within 1 month.
