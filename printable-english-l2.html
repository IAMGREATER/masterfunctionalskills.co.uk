1. Identifying Text Purpose
Reading & Analysis
P.A.F: Purpose • Audience • Form
Every text has a primary purpose: Persuade (sway opinions), Inform (factual details), Instruct (step-by-step directions), Explain (how/why something works), Advise (recommendations), or Entertain.
★ Golden Rule: Pair purpose with evidence (e.g. 'persuades via rhetorical questions').
⚠️ Watch Out: Identify the main purpose first — secondary intentions come second.

2. Audience & Tone Adaptation
Reading & Analysis
Who is the Reader?
Authors adapt their language, register, and layout for specific demographics: Professionals (formal jargon), Teenagers (casual, modern idioms), Parents, Patients, Customers.
★ Golden Rule: Determine audience by looking at formality, vocabulary, and pronouns.
⚠️ Watch Out: Avoid vague labels like 'general public' — name the exact audience.

3. Explicit vs Implicit (Inference)
Reading & Analysis
Fact Stated vs Reading Between Lines
Explicit = Directly and openly stated in the text word-for-word. Implicit = Hinted or suggested through clues, tone, and connotations without being said outright.
★ Golden Rule: Inference = Clues from text + Reader's logical deductions!
⚠️ Watch Out: Don't guess wild theories — every inference MUST be grounded in textual clues.

4. Fact vs Opinion vs Bias
Reading & Analysis
Verifiable Truth vs Subjective Belief
Fact: Can be proven true or false with objective evidence or data. Opinion: A personal view, feeling, or judgment. Bias: A one-sided presentation favouring one perspective.
★ Golden Rule: If it contains adjectives like 'finest', 'dreadful', or 'unacceptable', it is an OPINION!
⚠️ Watch Out: Watch out for opinions disguised as facts: 'Everyone knows London is the best city' is STILL an opinion.

5. Organisational Features & Effects
Reading & Analysis
Subheadings, Sidebars & Bullet Points
Always explain WHY an organisational feature is used: Subheadings (break text into chunks), Bullet points (isolate key items), Bold text (draws eye to essential warnings), Tables (organise data).
★ Golden Rule: Always explain HOW the feature guides the reader (not just its name)!
⚠️ Watch Out: Say 'draws reader's eye to safety steps', never just 'looks nice'.

6. Structural Signposting
Reading & Analysis
Chronological • Cause-Effect • Problem-Solution
Structure is how the writer organizes their points over time: Problem-Solution, Cause-and-Effect, Chronological narrative, or Thematic. Look for discourse markers (Firstly, Conversely, Therefore).
★ Golden Rule: Track shifts in topic and tone from opening to conclusion!
⚠️ Watch Out: Structure means the ORDER and flow of ideas — not spelling or grammar.

7. Persuasive Devices (DAFOREST)
Reading & Analysis
The Master Persuasion Toolkit
D - Direct Address ('you'). A - Alliteration. F - Facts. O - Opinions. R - Rhetorical questions. E - Emotive language. S - Statistics (85%). T - Rule of Three (triples).
★ Golden Rule: Quote the exact phrase, name the DAFOREST device, and explain how it influences the reader!
⚠️ Watch Out: Don't just write 'it makes you want to read on' — explain that it creates guilt, urgency, or authority.

8. Figurative Language Devices
Reading & Analysis
Metaphor • Simile • Hyperbole
Simile: Compares using 'like' or 'as' ('busy as a bee'). Metaphor: States one thing IS another ('the hospital was a warzone'). Hyperbole: Deliberate dramatic exaggeration.
★ Golden Rule: Metaphors create vivid mental images by transferring qualities from one concept to another!
⚠️ Watch Out: Don't confuse similes and metaphors: similes use 'like' or 'as'; metaphors do not.

9. Analysing Tone and Register
Reading & Analysis
The Writer's Attitude & Mood
Tone is the emotion or attitude behind the words: Sarcastic, Urgent, Critical, Humorous, Sympathetic, Objective, or Authoritative. Proved by diction (choice of words).
★ Golden Rule: Use precise tone adjectives instead of basic words like 'happy' or 'angry'!
⚠️ Watch Out: Tone can change within a text — look at the overall prevailing attitude.

10. Comparing Purpose & Perspective
Reading & Analysis
Pearson Section B Master Technique
To compare two texts for top marks: 1. Identify where both authors agree or share common ground. 2. Contrast how their viewpoints differ. 3. Use comparative connectives ('In contrast', 'Similarly').
★ Golden Rule: Always refer to BOTH Text A and Text B in every comparative paragraph!
⚠️ Watch Out: Writing two separate summaries without comparing them will cost you half the marks.

13. Evaluating Source Credibility
Reading & Analysis
Authority • Date • Vested Interest
To evaluate reliability: 1. Author authority (doctor vs blogger). 2. Publication date (is it outdated?). 3. Commercial bias (is the writer selling a product?). 4. Corroboration (can claims be verified?).
★ Golden Rule: Sources with commercial or personal interests are likely biased!
⚠️ Watch Out: Check author credentials — official-looking sites can still be biased.

Reading & Analysis
Deduce Meaning from Context
When encountering unfamiliar technical terms or jargon: 1. Read the surrounding sentences for clues. 2. Break down prefixes and root words. 3. Substitute a synonym to check if it makes sense.

Reading & Analysis
Core Claim vs Examples

⚠️ Watch Out: Don't mistake a single supporting case study for the writer's overarching point.

16. Summarising Multiple Paragraphs
Reading & Analysis
Synthesise in Your Own Words
To summarise: 1. Extract the key takeaways across paragraphs. 2. Condense into concise bullet points or sentences. 3. Paraphrase using YOUR OWN WORDS — never copy chunks verbatim.
★ Golden Rule: Exams penalise mindless copying — rewording proves you understood the text!
⚠️ Watch Out: Leave out minor descriptive details, anecdotes, and redundant adjectives.

17. Irony, Satire & Sarcasm
Reading & Analysis
Saying the Opposite of What is Meant
Verbal Irony: When an author says the opposite of their true meaning for humorous or critical effect. Understatement: Deliberately minimising seriousness ('it's just a scratch' on a wrecked car).
★ Golden Rule: Check if praise is sincere or sarcastic by looking at the broader context!
⚠️ Watch Out: Don't take ironic statements literally — that leads to picking the exact opposite answer.

18. Golden Reading Method: Q-E-L
Reading & Analysis
Quote • Explain • Link
To score full marks on 3-mark and 4-mark reading questions: 1. QUOTE exact textual phrase in quotation marks. 2. EXPLAIN the underlying meaning in your own words. 3. LINK directly to the author's purpose or question.
★ Golden Rule: Keep quotes short (1 to 5 words) — never copy entire three-line sentences!
⚠️ Watch Out: A quote without explanation earns 0 or 1 mark; you must explain its impact.

19. Formal Letter Layout & Salutations
Writing & Formats
Addresses, Date & Salutation Rules
Sender address top right. Recipient address top left below date. Rule: Dear Sir/Madam ends with 'Yours faithfully'. Named person (Dear Mr Smith) ends with 'Yours sincerely'.
★ Golden Rule: Named = Yours sincerely; Unnamed (Dear Sir/Madam) = Yours faithfully!
⚠️ Watch Out: Never mix them: Named = Sincerely; Unknown = Faithfully.

20. Formal Report Structure
Writing & Formats
Title • Background • Findings • Recommendations
A professional report requires 5 distinct sections: 1. Title. 2. Introduction / Purpose. 3. Current Findings / Key Issues (with subheadings). 4. Conclusion. 5. Actionable Recommendations.
★ Golden Rule: Use four clear sections: Title, Intro, Findings, and Recommendations!
⚠️ Watch Out: Keep report tone strictly objective — state evidence, not emotions.

21. Article Writing (Newspaper/Magazine)
Writing & Formats
Headline • Strapline • Byline • Hook
Articles must feature: 1. Punchy Headline (alliteration/pun). 2. Byline ('By [Name]'). 3. Engaging hook opening paragraph. 4. Balanced body exploring perspectives. 5. Concluding thought-provoking call to action.

22. Speech & Presentation Writing
Writing & Formats
Welcome • Passion • Rhetoric • Final Appeal
Speeches are written to be spoken aloud: 1. Greet audience ('Good morning fellow council members'). 2. State clear conviction. 3. Use rhetorical devices (rule of 3, pause cues). 4. Close with a memorable final rallying call.

⚠️ Watch Out: Remember to thank the audience at the very end of the speech.

Writing & Formats
Subject Line • Professional Body • Sign-off
Email format: 1. Informative Subject line (concise, clear). 2. Professional salutation (Dear Mr Davis / Dear Team). 3. Purpose stated in opening sentence. 4. Clear bulleted action points. 5. Formal sign-off (Kind regards).

★ Golden Rule: Adapt vocabulary and formality to match professional discussions!
⚠️ Watch Out: Avoid slang terms ('gonna', 'cheers') during formal assessments.

52. SLC L2: Mediating & Reaching Consensus
Speaking & Listening
Synthesise • Mediate • Steer to Consensus
To excel in the Level 2 formal discussion (15 mins): synthesise different viewpoints, mediate disagreements constructively, challenge assumptions with evidence, and help steer the group to a shared conclusion.
★ Golden Rule: Acknowledge opposing views fairly before proposing a compromise!
⚠️ Watch Out: Never dismiss arguments outright — validate before reframing.
