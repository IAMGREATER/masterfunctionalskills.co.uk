Comparing Texts & Contrasting Perspectives: Rules, Worked Models & Exam Practice

Specification & Learning Outcomes

Candidates must demonstrate mastery of the following regulated Functional Skills English outcomes:

  • L1.10 / L2.12 – Compare information, ideas, and perspectives across multiple extended texts on the same topic.
  • L2.15 / L2.18 – Synthesize evidence from multiple sources, comparing how writers use language and tone to convey agreement or disagreement.

Golden Rules for Comparing Texts & Contrasting Perspectives

Golden Rule 1: Use Comparative Connectives to Link Texts Directly

Never write about Text A in one isolated paragraph and Text B in another. Weave them together in the same sentence using comparative discourse markers: 'Similarly', 'Both texts agree that', 'Whereas', 'In sharp contrast', 'On the other hand'.

Golden Rule / Technique: Point-by-point integration: [Text A states X + Quote], [whereas Text B argues Y + Quote].
Golden Rule 2: Compare Ideas, Attitudes & Language Methods

High-scoring Level 2 comparative answers contrast not only factual claims, but also the authors' underlying attitudes (optimistic vs sceptical) and how they use language (statistics vs emotive hyperbole).

Golden Rule / Technique: Contrast: 1. Factual Claims + Quotes | 2. Writer's Attitude/Tone | 3. Language Devices

Core Skill 1: Comparative Synthesis Structure for Level 2 Reading

How to secure full marks on 3-mark to 6-mark comparison questions.

  1. Identify the Shared Theme: Confirm the exact sub-topic both writers are discussing (e.g. cost of electric vans, impact of working from home on productivity).
  2. Spot Similarities (Points of Agreement): Use "Both Text A and Text B agree that..." or "Similarly, Text B also notes..." supported by a short quote from each text.
  3. Spot Differences (Points of Disagreement): Use "Whereas Text A claims..., Text B argues..." supported by a short quote from each text.
  4. Compare Tone & Language (Level 2 Bonus): Contrast how each writer expresses their view (e.g., "Text A uses formal statistics ('40% reduction'), whereas Text B uses emotive language ('catastrophic mistake').").

Core Skill 2: Side-by-Side Comparison: Band 1 (Capped) vs Band 3 (Full Marks)

See why isolated summaries lose marks and integrated comparisons win full marks.

Band 1: Isolated Summaries (1/4 Marks)

"Text A is about electric ambulances and says they cut emissions by 40%. Text B is an interview with a paramedic who talks about rural charging stations."
Why it fails: No comparative connectives, no direct contrast of viewpoints, and no language comparison.

Band 3: Integrated Synthesis (4/4 Marks)

"Whereas Text A takes an enthusiastic view of electric ambulances, using statistics to show they 'slash emissions by 40%', Text B takes a cautious, critical view, warning in an urgent tone that lacking rural chargers leaves crews 'vulnerable during surges'."

Core Skill 3: Cross-Referencing Three Texts (Synthesising Evidence)

How to handle questions that ask you to gather evidence across Text A, Text B, and Text C.

Some questions ask: "Using information from Text A, Text B, and Text C, give three reasons why..."

Crucial Rule: You MUST take at least one piece of evidence from each of the named texts and label them clearly (e.g., "From Text A: ... | From Text B: ... | From Text C: ..."). If you take all three points from Text A and ignore Text B and C, you can only score 1 mark out of 3!

Pearson Edexcel Step-by-Step Worked Models

Benchmark Exam Model 1 2 Marks (Pearson Edexcel Reading Level 1)

Vocational Context: Retail Store Technology Feedback

Read the two staff comments:
Text A (Supervisor): 'The new card-only self-service tills have cut lunchtime queue times in half and eliminated end-of-day cash counting errors.'
Text B (Cashier): 'Although queues move faster at lunchtime, elderly customers who budget with cash feel excluded and frustrated by the card-only rule.'
(a) Identify one similarity between Text A and Text B. [1 Mark]
(b) Identify one difference between their views. [1 Mark]
Step
Description / Working
Criteria
Step 1 Identify shared agreement
Both Text A and Text B agree that the new tills have made lunchtime queues faster.
Criteria: 1 Mark [A1 Similarity]
Step 2 Contrast their overall attitude toward going cashless
Text A views the card-only tills purely positively (eliminating cash counting errors), whereas Text B points out that elderly customers who rely on cash feel excluded and frustrated.
Criteria: 1 Mark [A1 Difference]
Final Accredited Response: (a) Similarity: Both agree that lunchtime queues move faster. | (b) Difference: Text A praises the card-only rule for stopping cash errors, whereas Text B criticises it for excluding elderly customers who pay with cash.
Marking Scheme Guidance & Assessment Criteria
Look for concession clauses starting with 'Although' — writers often agree with the other text in that opening clause before stating their disagreement!
Benchmark Exam Model 2 4 Marks (Pearson Edexcel Reading Level 2)

Vocational Context: Emergency Services Fleet Modernization

Read the two extracts:
Text A (Government Press Release): 'Our green fleet revolution will slash urban ambulance emissions by 40% and save £1.8m in diesel costs, with rapid hubs charging batteries in just 20 minutes.'
Text B (Paramedic Union Interview): 'While everyone wants cleaner air, rushing into electric ambulances is a reckless gamble. Rapid chargers are nonexistent in rural hills — what happens when a battery dies mid-emergency?'
Compare how the two writers convey their perspectives on electric ambulances. [4 Marks]
Step
Description / Working
Criteria
Step 1 Contrast overall stance and priorities with quotes
Both texts acknowledge the benefit of cleaner air; however, Text A enthusiastically supports electric ambulances to 'slash urban ambulance emissions by 40%', whereas Text B opposes a rushed rollout, calling it a 'reckless gamble' that threatens patient safety.
Criteria: 2 Marks [A1 Viewpoint & Quote Contrast]
Step 2 Contrast views on charging reliability and language used
Text A uses confident statistics ('save £1.8m', 'charging batteries in just 20 minutes') in a promotional tone. In contrast, Text B highlights rural infrastructure gaps ('nonexistent in rural hills') and uses emotive language and a rhetorical question ('what happens when a battery dies mid-emergency?') to provoke alarm.
Criteria: 2 Marks [A1 Language & Tone Contrast]
Final Accredited Response: While both writers support reducing emissions, Text A takes an optimistic, promotional view backed by statistics ('slash urban ambulance emissions by 40%', 'charging batteries in just 20 minutes'). Conversely, Text B adopts an urgent, critical stance, warning that rural chargers are 'nonexistent' and using emotive phrasing ('reckless gamble') and a rhetorical question ('what happens when a battery dies mid-emergency?') to highlight patient risk.
Marking Scheme Guidance & Assessment Criteria
Notice the use of 'while', 'however', and 'conversely' alongside direct quotations from both texts. This structure guarantees 4/4 marks.

Critical Examiner Traps & Common Pitfalls

Examiner Pitfall: Writing Two Separate Summaries with No Comparison
Summarizing Text A in one block and Text B in a separate block without linking connectives caps your score heavily.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Use comparative linking words ('whereas', 'in contrast', 'similarly') in every single point of your response.
Examiner Pitfall: Forgetting to Quote from BOTH Texts
If a Level 2 question asks you to compare how two texts convey their views and you only include quotations from Text A, you cannot achieve top-band marks.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Pair every Text A quotation with a matching Text B quotation in the same sentence.

Interactive Exam Practice Questions

Exam Practice Question 1 3 Marks
Read the two extracts on remote GP appointments:
Text A (Health App Brochure): 'Video consultations empower busy workers by eliminating travel time and cutting waiting room infection risks by 50%.'
Text B (Age Charity Report): 'Replacing face-to-face appointments with smartphone apps alienates elderly patients who lack digital skills or fast broadband.'
Compare the viewpoints and language of Text A and Text B. [3 Marks]
Marking Scheme Criteria:
1. Viewpoint contrast: Text A views video consultations positively as convenient and safe for busy workers ('eliminating travel time'), whereas Text B views them negatively as excluding older people ('alienates elderly patients') [2 Marks].
2. Language contrast: Text A uses positive verbs ('empower') and a statistic ('50%'), whereas Text B uses emotive vocabulary ('alienates') to highlight digital exclusion [1 Mark].
Final Model Answer: Text A presents video appointments positively as convenient and hygienic, using the verb 'empower' and a '50%' statistic. In contrast, Text B criticises them for excluding older people without broadband, using the emotive verb 'alienates'.
Examiner Tip: Directly balance the two viewpoints and quote key words ('empower' vs 'alienates') in a single coherent answer.
Exam Practice Question 2 3 Marks
Read the two extracts on a proposed four-day working week (32 hours for 100% pay):
Text A (HR Trial Summary): 'During our six-month four-day week trial, staff sick leave dropped by 62% and overall company revenue rose by 8%.'
Text B (Retail Franchise Owner): 'Four-day weeks may work for desk jobs, but small shops open seven days a week would be forced to hire extra part-time staff and go bankrupt!'
Compare how the two writers present the four-day working week.
Marking Scheme Criteria:
1. Stance comparison: Text A supports the four-day week as a proven success for wellbeing and profits, whereas Text B opposes it as financially impossible for small seven-day retail shops [2 Marks].
2. Evidence/Tone comparison: Text A relies on measured trial statistics ('sick leave dropped by 62%', 'revenue rose by 8%'), whereas Text B uses dramatic, emotive hyperbole ('go bankrupt!') [1 Mark].
Final Model Answer: Text A supports the four-day week using positive trial statistics ('sick leave dropped by 62%', 'revenue rose by 8%'). Conversely, Text B argues it only suits desk jobs and uses emotive exaggeration ('go bankrupt!') to warn that small shops cannot afford extra staffing costs.
Examiner Tip: Contrast the statistical evidence in Text A against the emotional warning in Text B.
Exam Practice Question 3 3 Marks
Using information from all three extracts below, give three reasons why employees are leaving city-centre offices:
Text A: 'Annual rail season tickets rose by 6.5% in March, costing commuters over £3,800.'
Text B: 'Parents save 90 minutes a day on school-run travel when working from home.'
Text C: 'City-centre air quality breached safe nitrogen dioxide limits on 42 days last year.'
Marking Scheme Criteria:
1. From Text A: High commuting costs (rail season tickets rose by 6.5% to over £3,800) [1 Mark].
2. From Text B: Better work-life balance / saving 90 minutes a day on school-run travel by working from home [1 Mark].
3. From Text C: Poor city-centre air quality / pollution (breached safe nitrogen dioxide limits on 42 days) [1 Mark].
Final Model Answer: 1. (Text A) Expensive rail fares (£3,800 / up 6.5%). | 2. (Text B) Saving 90 minutes daily on school-run travel. | 3. (Text C) High city-centre air pollution (breached safe limits 42 times).
Examiner Tip: When a question names three texts, make sure you extract one distinct point from each text!
