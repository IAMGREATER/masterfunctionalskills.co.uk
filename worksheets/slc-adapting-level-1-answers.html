Section A: Core Skills (The Basics) Solutions Questions 1 & 2 • 4 Marks
Question 1 (Adapting Spoken Register from Casual Peer Chat to Formal Board Briefing) Model Solution [2 Marks Total]
Adapt this casual spoken remark to a colleague so that it is appropriate for a formal presentation to the NHS Trust Finance Director:
'The old printers on Ward 4 are proper rubbish and keep jamming up, so we're wasting loads of cash fixing them.'
Step-by-Step Marking Criteria:
1. Replace slang ('proper rubbish', 'loads of cash') with formal workplace diction ('unreliable', 'significant maintenance expenditure') [A1]
2. Frame as a clear, professional operational recommendation [A1]
Award Final Answer Mark: 'The legacy printers on Ward 4 are experiencing frequent mechanical jams, which is generating significant maintenance expenditure and delaying clinical admin.'
⚠️ Common Mistake to Avoid: Keeping colloquialisms like 'loads of' or 'rubbish' when addressing senior management.
🔄 Quick Check: Professional vocabulary, measured tone, zero slang.
Question 2 (Core Skill Application — 2 Marks) Model Solution [2 Marks Total]
Rewrite this casual spoken remark so it is appropriate for a formal presentation to your College Board of Governors:
'Loads of students are quitting the evening maths class cos the buses stop running at 8pm and they can't get home.'
Step-by-Step Marking Criteria:
1. Replace informal slang ('Loads of', 'quitting', 'cos') with formal educational register. [M1]
2. State the transport issue clearly and professionally. [A1]
Award Final Answer Mark: 'A significant number of adult learners have withdrawn from the evening Mathematics programme because local public bus services cease operating at 20:00, leaving students without reliable transport home.'
⚠️ Common Mistake to Avoid: Throwing around terms like 'SOP', 'KPI', 'DSE', or 'dyspnoea' when speaking to customers or patients creates confusion and fails audience adaptation criteria.
🔄 Quick Check: Replacing 'Loads of students are quitting cos...' with 'A significant number of adult learners have withdrawn because...' instantly elevates your spoken register.
Section B: Real-Life & Workplace Practice Solutions Questions 3 & 4 • 6 Marks
Question 3 (Model 1 (Level 1): Explaining a Vehicle Repair Delay to a Customer) Model Solution [3 Marks Total]
A mechanic tells you: 'Tell Mr Robinson his van won't be ready today because the supplier sent the wrong alternator bracket.'
Deliver a polite, 3-sentence spoken message to Mr Robinson that apologises, explains the delay clearly without blaming people, and confirms tomorrow's collection time.
Step-by-Step Marking Criteria:
1. Courteous greeting & sincere apology: 'Good afternoon Mr Robinson, I am calling from Metro Garage with an update on your transit van, and I want to apologise for a slight delay.' [A1]
2. Clear explanation & new completion time: 'Unfortunately, the replacement alternator bracket delivered this morning did not match your engine model, so we have ordered an express replacement arriving at 08:30 tomorrow.' [A1]
3. Reassurance & customer check: 'Our senior technician will fit and road-test it first thing, meaning your van will be ready for collection by 11:00 tomorrow morning—does that arrangement work for you?' [A1]
Award Final Answer Mark: 'Good afternoon Mr Robinson, I am calling from Metro Garage with an update on your van, and I sincerely apologise for a slight delay. Unfortunately, the alternator bracket delivered this morning did not match your engine model, so we have arranged an express replacement arriving at 08:30 tomorrow. Our technician will fit and test it immediately, so your van will be ready for collection by 11:00 tomorrow morning.'
⚠️ Common Mistake to Avoid: Either use plain English from the start or immediately define the term.
🔄 Quick Check: Always pair bad news (a delay) with a definite solution and an exact updated time ('ready by 11:00 tomorrow').
Question 4 (Exam-Style Task — 3 Marks) Model Solution [3 Marks Total]
An IT technician needs to tell a non-technical receptionist: 'Don't click links in phishing emails because malware will encrypt the local LAN server.'
Rewrite this spoken advice in 2–3 clear, jargon-free sentences that explain what to look out for and why it matters.
Step-by-Step Marking Criteria:
1. Explain 'phishing emails' in plain terms (suspicious emails pretending to be from banks or couriers). [M1]
2. Explain 'malware encrypting the LAN server' in plain terms (harmful software locking all our patient appointment files). [A1]
Award Final Answer Mark: 'Please be extra cautious if you receive unexpected emails asking you to click a link or enter your password, even if they look official. Clicking a fake link can install harmful software that locks everyone out of our shared patient booking computer system. If an email ever looks unusual, please forward it straight to the IT desk before clicking anything.'
⚠️ Common Mistake to Avoid: Reciting memorised dictionary words that you wouldn't naturally use makes your delivery sound stiff and insincere.
🔄 Quick Check: Explaining the practical consequence ('locks everyone out of our booking system') helps non-technical colleagues understand why the rule matters.
