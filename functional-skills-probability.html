Specification & Learning Outcomes

Candidates must demonstrate mastery of the following regulated Functional Skills Mathematics outcomes:

  • L1.26 / L2.33 – Express probabilities of single events as fractions, decimals, or percentages on a scale from 0 (impossible) to 1 (certain).
  • L2.34 – Use the property that the probabilities of all mutually exclusive outcomes sum to 1.

Golden Rules for Probability Scale & Single Event Outcomes

Golden Rule 1: P(Event) = Favourable Outcomes ÷ Total Possible Outcomes

Probability is ALWAYS a number between 0 and 1 (or 0% and 100%). Write probabilities as a fraction (e.g. 3/10), a decimal (0.3), or a percentage (30%)—NEVER as a ratio (3 : 10) or words ("3 out of 10")!

Golden Rule / Method: P(A) = Successful Outcomes ÷ Total Outcomes (Fraction, Decimal, or %)
Golden Rule 2: Complement Rule — Mutually Exclusive Probabilities Sum to 1

If an event either happens or does not happen, their probabilities add up to 1 (or 100%). Therefore: P(Not A) = 1 − P(A).

Golden Rule / Method: P(Not A) = 1 − P(A)

Core Skill 2: Mutually Exclusive Probability Tables (Sum = 1)

Finding missing probabilities when all possible outcomes are listed.

When a table lists every mutually exclusive outcome (e.g. Early = 0.15, On Time = 0.72, Late = ?):

  • Add the known probabilities: 0.15 + 0.72 = 0.87.
  • Subtract from 1.00: P(Late) = 1.00 − 0.87 = 0.13.
Level 1 Level 2 Pearson Edexcel City & Guilds NCFE

Core Skill 3: Experimental Probability (Relative Frequency) & Sample Size

Why larger trial counts produce more reliable probability estimates.

  • Relative Frequency Formula: Number of observed times event occurred ÷ Total number of trials conducted.
  • Reliability Rule: Increasing the number of trials (e.g. testing 500 items instead of 20 items) makes the experimental probability much more reliable.
Level 1 Level 2 Pearson Edexcel City & Guilds NCFE

Pearson Edexcel Step-by-Step Worked Models

Benchmark Exam Model 1 3 Marks (Pearson Edexcel Level 1/2)

Vocational Context: Transfusion Laboratory Blood Group Storage

A hospital blood bank refrigerator stores 50 units of blood:
• Group O: 22 units | Group A: 18 units | Group B: 7 units | Group AB: 3 units
One unit is selected at random.
(a) What is the probability that the unit is Group O? Give your answer as a simplified fraction.
(b) What is the probability that the unit is NOT Group AB? Give your answer as a decimal.
Step
Description / Working
Criteria
Step 1 Calculate P(Group O) and simplify fraction
P(Group O) = 22 / 50 = 11 / 25.
Criteria: M1 / A1: Simplified fraction (11/25)
Step 2 Calculate P(Group AB) as a decimal
P(Group AB) = 3 ÷ 50 = 0.06.
Criteria: M1: Decimal conversion (0.06)
Step 3 Subtract from 1 for P(NOT Group AB)
1 − 0.06 = 0.94 (or 47 ÷ 50 = 0.94).
Criteria: A1: Accurate decimal answer (0.94)
Final Accredited Answer: (a) 11/25 | (b) 0.94
Method Checklist & Marking Scheme Guidance
Never write probability as a ratio like "11 : 25"—always write 11/25, 0.44, or 44%.
Benchmark Exam Model 2 4 Marks (Pearson Edexcel Level 2)

Vocational Context: Medical Device Manufacturing QA

A quality engineer tests a random sample of 250 digital thermometers:
• 235 Pass Grade A, 9 Pass Grade B, and the rest Fail.
(a) Calculate the experimental probability (relative frequency) that a thermometer Fails, as a decimal.
(b) The factory manufactures a batch of 4,000 thermometers. Each failed thermometer costs £8.50 to scrap and replace. Calculate the expected cost of failed thermometers in this batch.
Step
Description / Working
Criteria
Step 1 Find number of failed thermometers in the 250 sample
Failed = 250 − (235 + 9) = 250 − 244 = 6 failed thermometers.
Criteria: M1: Sample failure count (6)
Step 2 Calculate relative frequency of failure
P(Fail) = 6 ÷ 250 = 0.024 (or 2.4%).
Criteria: A1: Accurate decimal probability (0.024)
Step 3 Calculate expected failures in 4,000 batch and multiply by £8.50
Expected failures = 0.024 × 4,000 = 96 thermometers.
Expected scrap cost = 96 × £8.50 = £816.00.
Criteria: M1 / A1: 96 units and £816.00 total cost
Final Accredited Answer: (a) P(Fail) = 0.024 | (b) £816.00 (96 expected failures)
Method Checklist & Marking Scheme Guidance
Expected Frequency = Probability × Batch Size (0.024 × 4,000 = 96 units).

Critical Examiner Traps & Common Pitfalls

Examiner Pitfall: Writing Probabilities as Ratios (e.g. 1 : 4) or Words ("1 in 5")
Pearson Edexcel mark schemes explicitly reject ratios (1 : 4) and verbal phrases ("likely" or "1 out of 5") when a numerical probability is requested.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Always write a fraction (1/5), a decimal (0.2), or a percentage (20%).
Examiner Pitfall: Giving a Probability Greater Than 1
Dividing Total by Favourable (e.g. 50 ÷ 22 = 2.27) produces a number greater than 1, which is impossible for any probability.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: The smaller number (part) always goes on top of the larger number (total).

Interactive Exam Practice Questions

Exam Practice Question 1 2 Marks
A medical supply drawer contains 15 blue tourniquets, 10 green tourniquets, and 5 yellow tourniquets. One is selected at random. What is the probability that it is green? Give your answer as a simplified fraction.
Marking Scheme Steps:
1. Total tourniquets = 15 + 10 + 5 = 30.
2. P(Green) = 10 / 30 = 1 / 3.
Final Correct Answer: 1/3
Examiner Tip: Add all categories first (15 + 10 + 5 = 30) to find the denominator.
Exam Practice Question 2 3 Marks
A staff charity spinner has four sections: £5 Prize (P = 0.25), £10 Prize (P = 0.10), £20 Prize (P = 3x), and No Prize (P = 10x). Calculate the value of x and the probability of winning a £20 Prize.
Marking Scheme Steps:
1. Sum of known probabilities = 0.25 + 0.10 = 0.35.
2. Remainder for 3x + 10x (13x) = 1.00 − 0.35 = 0.65.
3. x = 0.65 ÷ 13 = 0.05.
4. P(£20 Prize) = 3 × 0.05 = 0.15.
Final Correct Answer: x = 0.05; P(£20 Prize) = 0.15
Examiner Tip: All mutually exclusive sectors on a spinner must sum to 1.00.
Exam Practice Question 3 2 Marks
Two quality inspectors test batches from the same machine. Inspector A tests 20 items and finds 1 fault. Inspector B tests 500 items and finds 18 faults. Whose estimate of the fault probability is more reliable, and why?
Marking Scheme Steps:
1. Inspector B’s estimate (18/500 = 0.036) is more reliable.
2. Reason: Inspector B used a much larger sample size (500 trials vs 20 trials).
Final Correct Answer: Inspector B, because a larger sample size (500 vs 20) gives a more reliable estimate
Examiner Tip: In statistics, a larger sample size always gives a more reliable relative frequency.
