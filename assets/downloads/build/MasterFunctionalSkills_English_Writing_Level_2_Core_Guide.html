Pearson Edexcel Level 2 Writing • Specification Overview

▶Pearson Edexcel Level 2 Assessment Structure

Examination Structure: The Pearson Edexcel Functional Skills English Level 2 Writing examination is an externally assessed, 60-minute paper comprising two distinct writing tasks:

  • Task 1 (Extended Writing • 21 Marks • 35 Minutes): A high-challenge workplace, community, or professional scenario requiring an extended response of 250–350 words. Assessed across Composition & Effect (12 marks) and SPaG (9 marks).
  • Task 2 (Structured Writing • 15 Marks • 25 Minutes): A targeted functional response of 200–250 words to a specific brief. Assessed across Composition & Effect (9 marks) and SPaG (6 marks).

Total Marks: 36 Marks • SPaG Weighting: 41.7% (15 marks total). Candidates must demonstrate sophisticated register, complete paragraph organisation, and accurate complex punctuation.

✓Golden Examiner Rule
Cover every single bullet point in the question prompt. Missing a bullet point caps your Composition score at Band 2.
!Critical Audit Trap
Never use colloquial slang, unexpanded contractions (don't, can't), or informal text-speak in formal letters, reports, or articles.

              Comprehensive Functional Writing Glossary

              Active voice
              A sentence structure where the subject performs the action (e.g. 'The supervisor audited the records'). Compare with passive voice.
              Audience
              The intended readership of a text (e.g. senior manager, local council, general public). Dictates register, tone, and vocabulary.
              Byline
              A line in an article or newsletter naming the writer (e.g. 'By Alex Morgan, Community Liaison Officer').
              Cohesion
              The grammatical and lexical linking within a text that holds it together and makes ideas flow logically.
              Colloquialism
              Informal, conversational language or slang (e.g. 'kids', 'gross', 'a lot of hassle'). Avoid in formal writing.
              Colon
              A punctuation mark (:) used to introduce an explanation, expansion, or list of items.
              Comma splice
              A serious grammatical error where two independent clauses are joined with only a comma instead of a conjunction, semicolon, or full stop.
              Complex sentence
              A sentence containing one independent main clause and one or more dependent subordinate clauses.
              Compound sentence
              A sentence formed by joining two independent clauses with a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so).
              Conjunctive adverb
              An adverb that connects two clauses (e.g. 'furthermore', 'consequently', 'therefore', 'nevertheless'). Preceded by a semicolon.
              Contraction
              A shortened word form using an apostrophe of omission (e.g. 'don't', 'it's'). Generally avoided in formal reports and letters.
              Discourse markers
              Words or phrases that signpost the structure and direction of writing (e.g. 'In contrast', 'Furthermore', 'To summarise').
              Formal register
              A style of language characterized by standard English, complex sentences, objective tone, and absence of slang.
              Format markers
              Visual and structural layout features required by specific document types (e.g. addresses, subject lines, subheadings, bullet points).
              Imperative verb
              A bossy or commanding verb used to give instructions or advice (e.g. 'Wear safety goggles', 'Submit your form').
              Incident report
              A formal, objective document detailing the chronological events, causes, impacts, and actions following a workplace accident.
              Informal register
              A casual, conversational style suitable for personal emails or informal social forum posts.
              Inverted commas
              Quotation marks ('...' or "...") used to indicate direct speech, quotations, or specialist article headlines.
              Modal verb
              An auxiliary verb expressing possibility, necessity, or certainty (e.g. 'could', 'should', 'may', 'might', 'must'). Essential for professional hedging.
              Nominalisation
              Transforming verbs or adjectives into abstract nouns to create a more authoritative, formal tone (e.g. 'fail' -> 'failure', 'react' -> 'reaction').
              Passive voice
              A sentence structure where the object becomes the subject and receives the action (e.g. 'The audit was conducted by the supervisor'). Promotes objectivity.
              Purpose
              The primary communicative goal of writing (e.g. to inform, persuade, complain, explain, instruct, evaluate).
              Register
              The degree of formality and linguistic style adopted to suit a specific audience and context.
              Rhetorical question
              A question asked for effect or emphasis without expecting an answer, used to engage readers in articles and speeches.
              Salutation
              The opening greeting of a letter or email (e.g. 'Dear Mr Davies', 'Dear Sir or Madam', 'Dear Colleagues').
              Semicolon
              A punctuation mark (;) used to join two closely related independent clauses or to separate complex items in a list containing internal commas.
              Sign-off
              The closing valediction of a correspondence (e.g. 'Yours sincerely' when recipient is named, 'Yours faithfully' when unnamed).
              Standard English
              The widely accepted formal dialect of English taught in education and used in professional business, law, and publishing.
              Subordinate clause
              A dependent clause that adds detail to a main clause but cannot stand alone as a grammatical sentence.
              Topic sentence
              The opening sentence of a paragraph that introduces its central theme or main idea.
