Core Writing Strategy & Proofreading

▶The Four Pillars of Band 4 Exam Execution
1. Decoding Prompts (The TAP Method) & Register
• T (Text Format): Identify exact layout markers (addresses, To/From/Subject, headline, subheadings, or speech greeting).
• A (Audience): Authority figure (manager, council, director) = Formal (no contractions, objective tone). Friend/peer = Informal (warm, conversational). Public = Semi-formal.
• P (Purpose): Inform, Persuade, Advise, Explain, or Complain.
• Sign-Off Rules: Dear Sir/Madam → Yours faithfully | Dear Mr/Ms [Surname] → Yours sincerely | Hi/Dear [First Name] → Kind regards / Best wishes.
2. The 5-Minute Planning & Paragraph Formula
• 5-Min Plan: Sketch a 4-Box Grid (TAP + 3 Bullet Paragraphs + 5 Specialist Words + Punctuation Targets) or Spider Diagram.
• When to Start a New Paragraph (TiPToP): Change of Time, Place, Topic (each exam bullet = 1 dedicated paragraph), or Person.
• P.E.E. Paragraph Structure: Point (topic sentence) → Evidence/Example (fact, statistic, or case detail) → Explanation (impact or action).
• Corrections: Cross out errors with a single neat line and write clearly above.
3. Persuasive Toolkit (DAFOREST) & Opinions
• Direct Address (you, our community) • Alliteration (practical, proven progress)
• Facts & Statistics (74% of surveyed residents confirmed...)
• Opinions backed by evidence & balanced counter-arguments (While some argue..., the evidence proves...)
• Rhetorical Questions (Can we truly afford to ignore this risk?)
• Emotive Language (vital, unacceptable) • Triplets / Rule of Three (fair, affordable, and sustainable).
4. The 5-Minute Targeted Proofreading Routine
• Scan 1 (Sentence Boundaries): Check every sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with a full stop, question mark, or semicolon—never a comma splice!
• Scan 2 (Grammar & Tense): Verify consistent past/present tense and subject-verb agreement (we were, never we was; could have, never could of).
• Scan 3 (Homophones & Spelling): Audit their/there/they're, your/you're, to/too/two, affect/effect, practice/practise, advice/advise, and double-letter words (accommodation, necessary, recommend).

Format 1: Article (Magazine, Newspaper & Web)

★What Is an Article? Purpose, Audience & Register Rules

An article is a public-facing piece of journalism written for a newspaper, magazine, staff bulletin, or community website. Its purpose is to inform, engage, and persuade a broad readership about a topical issue, controversy, or community trend. Unlike a dry business report, an article must hook the reader immediately with a compelling headline, sustain interest using rhetorical devices (DAFOREST), explore multiple perspectives with clear subheadings, and finish with a memorable verdict or call to action.

Target Audience: General public, local residents, students, or readers of a community/workplace magazine.

Required Tone & Register: Semi-formal, lively, persuasive, balanced, and authoritative (use standard English with journalistic flair; avoid slang).

Essential Layout Features: Catchy headline, byline (By [Name]), engaging opening hook, 2–3 thematic subheadings, balanced body paragraphs with rhetorical devices, decisive concluding call to action.

Unit 1: Magazine & Web Feature Article
▶Step-by-Step Structural Anatomy (Top to Bottom) & Specialist Vocabulary
1. Catchy Headline
Use alliteration, a colon, or a provocative question to hook the reader immediately (e.g. 'Flexible Working: Helping or Hurting Our Town?').
2. Professional Byline
Place your name and role directly beneath the headline (e.g. 'By Alex Morgan | Community Correspondent').
3. Introductory Hook
Open with a striking trend, rhetorical question, or relatable community observation that defines the issue.
4. Thematic Subheadings
Break the body into 2–3 clear sections (e.g. 'Benefits for Workers', 'Challenges for the High Street').
5. Persuasive Evidence (DAFOREST)
Integrate statistics, expert viewpoints, rule of three, and discourse markers ('Furthermore', 'On the other hand').
6. Verdict & Call to Action
End with a forward-looking summary, practical compromise, or direct challenge to the reader.

Flexible Working: Is It Helping or Hurting Our Community?
By Alex Morgan | Community Features Correspondent

Over the last few years, the way we work has changed completely. Many people in Greendale now work from home or do hybrid working hours instead of travelling to an office every day. But is this new routine good for our town, or is it creating unexpected challenges?

The Benefits for Workers

Firstly, flexible working has significantly improved people's work-life balance. Without a stressful daily commute, workers spend more time with family, exercise, and cook healthy meals. Many residents report that their mental wellbeing is much better. In addition, people say their productivity has increased because they have fewer noisy distractions at home.

Challenges for Local Shops and Transport

On the other hand, there are clear drawbacks. Some workers experience isolation at home without colleagues. Furthermore, local businesses and town-centre cafes are losing money because fewer commuters buy lunch or coffee. Buses and trains are also less full, which could lead to reduced transport infrastructure in the future.

What Support Is Needed?

To make this system sustainable for everyone, our council and employers must take action. The council should open shared workspace facilities in the library with fast internet and meeting rooms. Meanwhile, employers must provide safe ergonomic equipment and check on staff regularly.

In conclusion, flexible working is here to stay. If we work together to support both remote workers and local businesses, our whole community will benefit.

Format 2: Email (Workplace, Formal & Informal)

★What Is an Email? Purpose, Audience & Register Rules

An email is the primary channel for rapid written communication in modern workplaces and everyday life. In the Level 2 exam, you may be asked to write a formal workplace email (to a manager, client, or council officer) or a semi-formal/informal email (to a colleague or friend). Every email must display a clear header block (To, From/Date, and a concise Subject line), an appropriate salutation matching your relationship with the recipient, focused paragraphs (often using bullet points for key options or dates), a clear next step, and a matching sign-off.

Target Audience: Workplace manager, colleague, external client, or friend (check the prompt carefully to choose formal vs informal register).

Required Tone & Register: Formal & professional for managers/clients ('Dear Ms Davies' -> 'Kind regards' / 'Yours sincerely'); warm & polite for colleagues/friends ('Hi Sam' -> 'Best wishes').

Essential Layout Features: Email header block (To:, From:, Subject:), appropriate salutation, opening purpose sentence, structured paragraphs/bullets, action request, matching sign-off & job title.

Unit 2: Workplace Proposal Email
▶Step-by-Step Structural Anatomy (Top to Bottom) & Specialist Vocabulary
1. Email Header Block
Always write 'To:', 'From:' (or 'Date:'), and a specific, informative 'Subject:' line at the top.
2. Calibrated Salutation
Formal: 'Dear Mr/Ms [Surname],' or 'Dear [First Name],' for a known manager. Informal: 'Hi [Name],'.
3. Immediate Purpose Line
State why you are emailing in your first sentence ('I am writing to summarise the team feedback...').
4. Structured Body & Bullets
Organise feedback, costs, and logistical details into clear paragraphs or concise bullet points.
5. Clear Action / Next Step
Specify what approval, reply, or action you need from the recipient and by when.
6. Matching Sign-Off & Role
Close with 'Kind regards,' or 'Yours sincerely,' (formal) or 'Best wishes,' (informal), plus your full name and title.

Format 3: Formal & Informal Letter

★What Is a Formal & Informal Letter? Purpose, Audience & Register Rules

A letter is the gold-standard format for official correspondence, including formal complaints, job applications, council proposals, and tenancy notices, as well as personal letters to friends or relatives. Pearson Edexcel examiners strictly assess letter layout conventions: a formal letter requires the sender's address and date in the top-right corner, the recipient's name and address on the left, a formal salutation, a bold 'Re:' subject line, chronological evidence, a clear request for redress/action, and the correct pairing of salutation and valediction (sign-off).

Target Audience: General Manager, Council Director, Landlord, or Employer (Formal); Friend or Family Member (Informal — sender address + date only, 'Dear [Name]' -> 'Best wishes').

Required Tone & Register: Formal Letter: Polite, assertive, objective, and unemotional (no contractions). Informal Letter: Warm, conversational, and personal.

Essential Layout Features: Sender address & date (top right), recipient name & address (left, formal only), salutation, 'Re:' subject line, chronological body paragraphs, resolution request, correct sign-off.

Unit 3: Formal Complaint Letter
▶Step-by-Step Structural Anatomy (Top to Bottom) & Specialist Vocabulary
1. Sender Address & Date (Top Right)
Write your street, town, and postcode in the top-right corner, with the full date directly underneath (never put your name here).
2. Recipient Address (Top Left)
For formal letters, write the recipient's name/job title, organisation, and address on the left margin below the date line.
3. Salutation & Sign-Off Rule
'Dear Sir/Madam,' MUST end with 'Yours faithfully,'. 'Dear Mr/Ms [Surname],' MUST end with 'Yours sincerely,'.
4. Subject Line (Re: ...)
Include a concise subject heading under the greeting (e.g. 'Re: Formal Complaint – Booking Ref #HV-4092').
5. Factual Evidence & Impact
Present events in logical order with exact dates, times, and booking references, explaining the real-world impact.
6. Specific Redress & Deadline
State clearly what resolution or refund you expect and set a reasonable timeframe (e.g. 'within 14 days').

dissatisfactionsubstandardunacceptablepre-bookedinconveniencecompensationresolutiondismissiveunderstaffedremedial actionreimbursementchronological
✓Tutor Golden Rule
Memorise the Salutation Golden Rule: 'Dear Sir/Madam' (name unknown) = 'Yours faithfully'. 'Dear Mr Vance' (named person) = 'Yours sincerely'. For an informal letter to a friend, omit the recipient's left-hand address and close with 'Best wishes,'.
!Examiner Red Flag
Never write your own name at the top of your sender address in the top-right corner, and never mix up 'Yours faithfully' and 'Yours sincerely'.

A speech or presentation talk is a spoken-style script written to be delivered aloud to a live audience—such as colleagues at a staff briefing, residents at a community meeting, or students at an induction day. Comprehensive Functional Writing Glossary (A–C)

Active voice
A sentence structure where the subject performs the action (e.g. 'The supervisor audited the records'). Compare with passive voice.
Audience
The intended readership of a text (e.g. senior manager, local council, general public). Dictates register, tone, and vocabulary.
Byline
A line in an article or newsletter naming the writer (e.g. 'By Alex Morgan, Community Liaison Officer').
Cohesion
The grammatical and lexical linking within a text that holds it together and makes ideas flow logically.
Colloquialism
Informal, conversational language or slang (e.g. 'kids', 'gross', 'a lot of hassle'). Avoid in formal writing.
Colon
A punctuation mark (:) used to introduce an explanation, expansion, or list of items.
Comma splice
A serious grammatical error where two independent clauses are joined with only a comma instead of a conjunction, semicolon, or full stop.
Complex sentence
A sentence containing one independent main clause and one or more dependent subordinate clauses.
Compound sentence
A sentence formed by joining two independent clauses with a coordinating conjunction (FANBOYS: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so).
Conjunctive adverb
An adverb that connects two clauses (e.g. 'furthermore', 'consequently', 'therefore', 'nevertheless'). Preceded by a semicolon.

Functional Writing Glossary (C–N)

Contraction
A shortened word form using an apostrophe of omission (e.g. 'don't', 'it's'). Generally avoided in formal reports and letters.
Discourse markers
Words or phrases that signpost the structure and direction of writing (e.g. 'In contrast', 'Furthermore', 'To summarise').
Formal register
A style of language characterized by standard English, complex sentences, objective tone, and absence of slang.
Format markers
Visual and structural layout features required by specific document types (e.g. addresses, subject lines, subheadings, bullet points).
Imperative verb
A bossy or commanding verb used to give instructions or advice (e.g. 'Wear safety goggles', 'Submit your form').
Incident report
A formal, objective document detailing the chronological events, causes, impacts, and actions following a workplace accident.
Informal register
A casual, conversational style suitable for personal emails or informal social forum posts.
Inverted commas
Quotation marks ('...' or "...") used to indicate direct speech, quotations, or specialist article headlines.
Modal verb
An auxiliary verb expressing possibility, necessity, or certainty (e.g. 'could', 'should', 'may', 'might', 'must'). Essential for professional hedging.
Nominalisation
Transforming verbs or adjectives into abstract nouns to create a more authoritative, formal tone (e.g. 'fail' -> 'failure', 'react' -> 'reaction').

Functional Writing Glossary (P–T)

Passive voice
A sentence structure where the object becomes the subject and receives the action (e.g. 'The audit was conducted by the supervisor'). Promotes objectivity.
Purpose
The primary communicative goal of writing (e.g. to inform, persuade, complain, explain, instruct, evaluate).
Register
The degree of formality and linguistic style adopted to suit a specific audience and context.
Rhetorical question
A question asked for effect or emphasis without expecting an answer, used to engage readers in articles and speeches.
Salutation
The opening greeting of a letter or email (e.g. 'Dear Mr Davies', 'Dear Sir or Madam', 'Dear Colleagues').
Semicolon
A punctuation mark (;) used to join two closely related independent clauses or to separate complex items in a list containing internal commas.
Sign-off
The closing valediction of a correspondence (e.g. 'Yours sincerely' when recipient is named, 'Yours faithfully' when unnamed).
Standard English
The widely accepted formal dialect of English taught in education and used in professional business, law, and publishing.
Subordinate clause
A dependent clause that adds detail to a main clause but cannot stand alone as a grammatical sentence.
Topic sentence
The opening sentence of a paragraph that introduces its central theme or main idea.
