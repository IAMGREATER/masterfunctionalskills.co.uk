Reading Topic 1: Fact or Opinion

Fact or Opinion: Evaluating objective facts versus promotional claims on a community gym flyer.
★DfE Criteria & Core Concept

Curriculum Standard:
• L1.13 / L2.11: Identify and distinguish between fact and opinion in workplace, instructional, and informative texts.
• L2.14: Recognise bias, detect opinions disguised as facts, and determine whether statements represent verifiable truth or subjective evaluation.

Key Concept: A fact is verifiable using data, records, dates, measurements, or official statistics. If a statement can be checked against independent records and proven true or false, it is a fact — even if it sounds surprising.

✓Golden Rule
Opinions reflect personal feelings, beliefs, predictions, or recommendations. Look out for evaluative adjectives ('superb', 'appalling', 'vital', 'unfair') and modal verbs ('should', 'ought to').
!Examiner Trap
Just because a senior doctor or chief executive says 'This is the best ward management app available', that does not make the claim itself a fact. It remains an informed professional opinion.

Reading Topic 2: Words in Context & Meaning

Words in Context: Using surrounding sentences to decode unfamiliar words on an office notice.
★DfE Criteria & Core Concept

Curriculum Standard:
• L1.10 / L2.10: Understand vocabulary and determine how meaning is conveyed through word choice in context.
• L1.9 / L2.11: Identify and explain implicit (suggested) and explicit (stated) meanings across extended workplace texts.

Key Concept: When asked to give a word or phrase that can 'replace' a target word in the text, your answer MUST match the exact part of speech and verb tense (e.g., if the target word ends in '-ed' or '-ing', your replacement must also fit grammatically into the original sentence).

✓Golden Rule
Explicit meaning is stated directly on the page. Implicit meaning is hinted at between the lines — you must deduce it from clues, tone, and actions.
!Examiner Trap
A word like 'reservations' can mean 'restaurant bookings' or 'doubts/concerns'. Giving 'bookings' when the text says 'The union had reservations about the contract' scores 0.

Reading Topic 3: Specialist & Technical Vocabulary

Specialist Vocabulary: Using in-text brackets and context clues to define commercial kitchen terms.
★DfE Criteria & Core Concept

Curriculum Standard:
• L1.11 / L2.10: Understand and explain specialist and technical vocabulary in vocational and regulatory texts.
• L2.16: Recognise how glossaries, parenthetical definitions, and prefixes/suffixes clarify technical terms for different audiences.

Key Concept: Informational texts frequently define technical acronyms or specialist terms immediately upon first mention using brackets, dashes, or appositive commas (e.g., 'tachycardia — an abnormally rapid heart rate — must be logged'). Always check adjacent punctuation!

✓Golden Rule
When no glossary is given, split compound specialist words into familiar building blocks (e.g., 'anti-' = against, 'co-' = together, 'mal-' = bad/faulty, 'cardio-' = heart, 'therm-' = heat, 'musculo-skeletal' = muscles and bones).
!Examiner Trap
Candidates sometimes guess what an acronym like 'MAR' or 'PPE' stands for using everyday slang instead of scanning the text or footer glossary where the full expansion ('Medication Administration Record') is printed.

Reading Topic 4: Presentational Features

Presentational Features: Using bold headings, boxes, and bullet points on a clinic appointment letter.
★DfE Criteria & Core Concept

Curriculum Standard:
• L1.14 / L2.16: Recognise and explain how presentational and organisational features aid understanding and convey information.
• L2.17: Analyse how formatting, typographic emphasis, and visual signposts support the writer's overall purpose.

Key Concept: You only get half the marks for naming a feature (e.g. 'bullet points' or 'bold heading'). To score full marks, state WHAT specific information the feature highlights in that text and HOW it helps the reader.

✓Golden Rule
Presentational features are visual formatting elements you can see without reading the words (bold, bullet points, subheadings, boxes, photos). Language features are word choices (metaphors, rhetorical questions, rule of three).
!Examiner Trap
Alliteration, rhetorical questions, and metaphors are language techniques. Bold fonts, tables, bullet points, subheadings, and shaded boxes are presentational features.

Reading Topic 5: Layout Devices & Visual Signposting

Layout Devices: Navigating subheadings, numbered lists, and sidebars on a staff intranet FAQ page.
★DfE Criteria & Core Concept

Curriculum Standard:
• L1.14 / L2.16: Understand how layout and organisational devices structure complex information for rapid retrieval.
• L2.17: Analyse the relationship between textual hierarchy, columns, tables, sidebars, and reader navigation.

Key Concept: Page layout is engineered so the primary message occupies top prominence (banner headline), secondary themes are divided into columns or sub-sections, and reference details sit in sidebars or footers.

✓Golden Rule
Whenever a writer puts times, prices, dosages, or contact numbers into a table or sidebar instead of a paragraph, their goal is to enable instant cross-referencing without scanning walls of prose.
!Examiner Trap
If asked about layout, summarizing the medical facts or repeating the opening hours scores 0. You must discuss columns, sidebars, tables, spacing, and visual hierarchy.

Reading Topic 7: Language Devices & Rhetorical Techniques

Language Devices: Spotting rule of three, rhetorical questions, and direct address in a cycling flyer.
★DfE Criteria & Core Concept

Curriculum Standard:
• L1.12 / L2.13: Identify language techniques and explain how they convey meaning and influence the reader.

Caption: A brief text explanation placed beneath an image, chart, or diagram.

Context: The surrounding words, sentences, or situation that gives a word or text its meaning.

Fact: A verifiable piece of information that can be proven true by records, measurements, or evidence.

Headline / Heading: The main title of an article or document designed to state the subject clearly.

Implicit Meaning: Information that is suggested or hinted at rather than directly stated.

Informative Text: A text whose main purpose is to give clear, factual information to the reader.

Reading Glossary (L–T)

Layout Feature: A visual or structural element (columns, bullet points, boxes) that organises content.

Opinion: A personal belief, attitude, or feeling that cannot be verified as an objective fact.

Persuasive Text: A text designed to convince the reader to take action or agree with a viewpoint.

Purpose: The main reason why the author wrote the text (to inform, instruct, explain, or persuade).

Retrieval: Finding and extracting specific facts, figures, or dates directly from a text.

Subheading: A smaller heading that divides an article or document into clear sections.

Tone: The mood, feeling, or attitude conveyed by the writer's choice of words.
