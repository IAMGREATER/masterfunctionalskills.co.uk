Core Writing Strategy & Proofreading

▶The Four Steps to Level 1 Exam Success
1. Decoding Questions (The TAP Method) & Register
• T (Text Format): Spot the required format features (2 addresses for a formal letter, To/Subject for an email, headline/subheadings for an article or leaflet).
• A (Audience): Manager, landlord, council, or company = Formal (polite, no slang). Friend or relative = Informal (warm, friendly).
• P (Purpose): Inform, Persuade, Advise, Explain, Apply, or Complain.
• Letter Sign-Off Rule: Dear Sir/Madam → Yours faithfully | Dear Mr/Mrs [Surname] → Yours sincerely.
2. The 5-Minute Plan & Paragraph Formula
• 5-Min Plan: Before writing, jot down your format layout, 2–3 ideas for each bullet point, and 5 key words to include.
• When to Start a New Paragraph (TiPToP): Change of Time, Place, Topic (give each exam bullet point its own paragraph!), or Person.
• Paragraph Shape: Start with a clear main sentence, add an example or reason (because...), and explain what should happen.
• Leave a Blank Line: Always leave a clear line space between paragraphs.
3. Making Your Writing Engaging & Persuasive
• Direct Address: Speak directly to the reader using you, your, we, and our community in leaflets, articles, and talks.
• Rhetorical Questions: Ask a question to hook the reader (Have you noticed how busy our park has become?).
• Facts & Examples: Give realistic details (times, prices, dates, and practical examples) to develop each bullet point.
• Rule of Three: Group three adjectives together (cleaner, safer, and more welcoming).
4. The 5-Minute Proofreading Routine
• Check 1 (Capitals & Full Stops): Check every sentence starts with a capital letter and ends with a full stop (.) or question mark (?). Check capital I and days/months!
• Check 2 (Tenses & Agreement): Check past/present tense and make sure you wrote we were / they were (never we was / they was).
• Check 3 (Homophones & Spellings): Check their/there/they're, your/you're, to/too/two, and key words (because, necessary, business, accommodation).

Format 1: Article (Community, Magazine & Web)

★What Is an Article? Purpose, Audience & Layout Rules

An article is written for a community newsletter, local newspaper, college magazine, or website. Its purpose is to inform, interest, and persuade readers about an everyday topic that matters to them. Unlike a letter or email, an article needs an eye-catching headline, a byline with your name, clear subheadings to break up your points, and an encouraging final sentence.

Target Audience: Local residents, families, and readers of a community newsletter or website.

Required Tone & Register: Semi-formal, lively, friendly, and persuasive (use standard English; avoid slang).

Essential Layout Features: Catchy headline, writer's byline (By [Name]), clear opening paragraph, 2 short subheadings, linking words, positive call to action.

Unit 1: Greener Town Action Article
▶Step-by-Step Structural Anatomy (Top to Bottom) & Specialist Vocabulary
1. Catchy Headline
Write a clear, interesting title at the top of the page (e.g. 'Making Greenwood Park Better for Everyone').
2. Writer's Byline
Put your name directly underneath the headline (e.g. 'By Sam Taylor | Local Resident').
3. Engaging Opening
Introduce the topic clearly and ask a question or state why the issue matters to readers.
4. Clear Subheadings
Use 2 short subheadings (e.g. 'Current Problems' and 'Ideas for Improvement') to organise your paragraphs.
5. Facts, Examples & Reasons
Explain each bullet point using linking words ('Firstly', 'However', 'In addition', 'Because').
6. Positive Call to Action
Finish with an encouraging final message telling readers what should happen next.

Making Greenwood Park Better for Everyone
By Sam Taylor | Local Resident

Greenwood Park is a wonderful green space at the heart of our community. Every week, local families, dog walkers, and children visit the park to exercise and enjoy the fresh air. However, have you noticed how run-down some areas have become recently?

Current Problems in the Park

Firstly, there is far too much litter on the grass because there are not enough rubbish bins near the main gates. Secondly, several wooden benches are broken, and the concrete footpaths are cracked and muddy. In addition, poor evening lighting makes the park feel unsafe during winter afternoons.

Ideas for Improvement

To solve these issues, the council should carry out regular maintenance and install extra recycling bins around the play area. Furthermore, building a free outdoor gym with simple fitness equipment would encourage adults and teenagers to stay active. Organising weekend football games or family picnic days would also bring neighbours together.

In conclusion, Greenwood Park is vital to our town. By upgrading the facilities now, we can make our park cleaner, safer, and more welcoming for everyone!

community: All the people living in a particular local area or town.
facilities: Buildings, rooms, or services provided for people to use.
equipment: The items or apparatus needed for an activity or sport.
litter: Rubbish such as paper, cans, and wrappers left lying on the ground.
recycling: Collecting waste materials like plastic and glass so they can be reused.
benches: Long outdoor seats for two or more people in a public space.
outdoor gym: Weatherproof exercise machines installed in a public park.
lighting: Streetlamps and path lights that keep public areas bright and safe.
footpaths: Paved walkways designed for pedestrians and pushchairs.
families: Parents, children, and relatives visiting a place together.
maintenance: Regular repair and upkeep work to keep a place in good condition.
welcoming: Friendly, pleasant, and inviting for visitors.

Format 2: Email (Workplace, Formal & Informal)

★What Is an Email? Purpose, Audience & Layout Rules

An email is the quickest way to share information, make requests, or suggest ideas at work, college, or in daily life. In a Pearson Edexcel Level 1 exam, you must include a clear 'To:' line and a specific 'Subject:' line at the top. Match your greeting and sign-off to the reader: use 'Dear [Name]' and 'Kind regards' for a manager or tutor, or 'Hi [Name]' and 'Best wishes' for a close friend.

Target Audience: Your manager or colleague (Workplace/Formal) or a friend/family member (Informal).

Required Tone & Register: Polite, helpful, clear, and professional (or warm and friendly for an email to a friend).

Essential Layout Features: To: and Subject: header lines, polite greeting (Dear [Name],), clear opening sentence, structured paragraphs, polite sign-off (Kind regards,).

Unit 2: Community Library Event Email
▶Step-by-Step Structural Anatomy (Top to Bottom) & Specialist Vocabulary
1. Recipient & Subject Header
Write 'To:' and a clear 'Subject:' line summarising what the email is about.
2. Appropriate Greeting
Use 'Dear Sandra,' or 'Dear Mr Patel,' for work/formal emails, or 'Hi Sam,' for a friend.
3. Purpose Opening Line
State why you are emailing in your very first sentence ('I am writing to share my ideas for...').
4. Middle Detail Paragraphs
Group your points into 2–3 short paragraphs covering every bullet point in the question.
5. Helpful Closing Line
Include a polite closing sentence ('Please let me know what you think of these ideas.').
6. Sign-Off & Full Name
Close with 'Kind regards,' or 'Yours sincerely,' and write your full name and job role.

Format 3: Formal & Informal Letter

★What Is a Formal & Informal Letter? Purpose, Audience & Layout Rules

A letter is one of the most frequently tested formats in Pearson Edexcel Level 1 exams. For a formal letter (such as a complaint, job application, or council request), you must put your own address and the date in the top-right corner, and the recipient's name and address on the left side above 'Dear...'. For an informal letter to a friend or relative, include only your own address and date in the top-right corner and close warmly with 'Best wishes,'.

Target Audience: Business Manager, Letting Agent, Council Officer, or Employer (Formal); Friend or Relative (Informal).

Required Tone & Register: Formal Letter: Polite, firm, factual, and serious. Informal Letter: Warm, friendly, and personal.

Essential Layout Features: Sender address & date (top right), recipient name & address (left, formal only), Dear [Name], Re: subject line, clear paragraphs, matching sign-off.

Unit 3: Heating Repair Letter
▶Step-by-Step Structural Anatomy (Top to Bottom) & Specialist Vocabulary
1. Sender Address & Date (Top Right)
Write your street, town, and postcode in the top-right corner, with today's date underneath.
2. Recipient Address (Left Side)
For formal letters, write the person's name, job title, company, and address on the left.
3. Salutation & Sign-Off Rule
'Dear Sir/Madam,' ends with 'Yours faithfully,'. 'Dear Mr Jenkins,' ends with 'Yours sincerely,'.
4. Clear Subject Line (Re: ...)
Write a one-line subject heading below the greeting (e.g. 'Re: Urgent Repairs at Flat 3B').
5. Detailed Body Paragraphs
Explain what happened, give exact examples, and describe how the issue affects you.
6. Clear Action & Deadline
State clearly what you want the reader to do and give a reasonable date for a reply.

Level 1 Functional Writing Glossary (A–C)

Address
The recipient and sender postal details placed at the top of a formal letter.
Adjective
A describing word that gives more detail about a noun (e.g. 'cold rain', 'heavy boxes').
Adverb
A word that describes a verb, indicating how, when, or where an action occurs (e.g. 'quickly', 'yesterday').
Apostrophe
A punctuation mark (') used to show omission (don't = do not) or possession (the manager's desk).
Article
A piece of writing for a newspaper, magazine, or community website with a headline, subheadings, and paragraphs.
Audience
The person or group of people you are writing for (e.g. a store manager, local council, or friend).
Bullet points
Symbols used to list key facts, actions, or recommendations clearly in reports, leaflets, and emails.
Byline
The line underneath an article headline that gives the writer's name (e.g. 'By Sam Taylor').
Capital letter
An uppercase letter used for the first word in a sentence, proper nouns (names, days, places), and 'I'.
Comma
A punctuation mark (,) used to separate items in a list or mark a brief pause after a starter clause.

▶Final Level 1 Examination Commitments

Before entering the examination room, confirm your commitment to these five essential Level 1 standards:

  • 100% Sentence Punctuation: Every sentence begins with a capital letter and ends with a full stop, question mark, or exclamation mark.
  • Clear Paragraphing: Every bullet point in the question is developed into its own clear paragraph separated by a blank line.
  • Correct Format Layout: Letters have addresses and matching sign-offs; emails have To/Subject lines; articles, reports, and leaflets have clear headings.
  • Joined Complex Sentences: Ideas are linked smoothly using joining words such as because, although, however, and in addition.
  • 5-Minute Final Check: Reserve the final 5 minutes of each task to check capital 'I', proper nouns, homophones, and everyday spellings.
