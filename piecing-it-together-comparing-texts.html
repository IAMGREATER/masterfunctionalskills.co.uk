Specification & Learning Outcomes

Candidates must demonstrate mastery of the following regulated Functional Skills English outcomes:

  • L1.10 / L2.12 – Compare information, ideas, and opinions in different texts, identifying similarities and differences.
  • L2.15 – Analyse how two writers use different language, tone, and structural styles to convey contrasting perspectives.

Golden Rules for Comparing Texts & Cross-Text Synthesis

Golden Rule 1: Always Anchor Comparisons with Connectives

Never write about Text A in isolation and then Text B in isolation. Weave them together in the same sentence or paired sentences using comparative connectives ('similarly', 'in contrast', 'whereas', 'on the other hand').

Golden Rule / Technique: Formula: [Text A Point + Quote] + [Comparative Connective] + [Text B Point + Quote]
Golden Rule 2: Compare Like-with-Like Across Three Dimensions

Compare the same theme across both texts (e.g. cost vs cost, safety vs safety). In Level 2, check whether the question asks you to compare WHAT the texts say (content/ideas) or HOW they say it (tone/language/layout).

Golden Rule / Technique: Content (What is said) • Tone (Attitude) • Methods (Language & Layout)

Core Skill 1: The Cross-Text Synthesis Matrix

The rapid planning grid to structure 3-mark to 6-mark comparative exam answers.

Before writing a multi-mark comparison answer, mentally map how the two documents differ across core dimensions:

Comparison Dimension Text A (e.g. Official Corporate Policy) Text B (e.g. Staff Forum / Union Blog)
Purpose & Stance To inform and mandate compliance; positive or neutral institutional stance. To critique or share frontline frustrations; sceptical, urgent, or campaigning stance.
Tone & Register Formal, objective, detached, third-person register. Informal or passionate, subjective, first-person ('we', 'our') and direct address ('you').
Evidence Used Audit percentages, financial projections, legal statutes (HSE, CQC). Personal lived anecdotes, rhetorical questions, emotive case examples.
Level 1 Level 2 Pearson Edexcel City & Guilds NCFE

Core Skill 2: The Comparative Connective Bank (Similarities vs Differences)

High-impact discourse markers that signal direct cross-text synthesis to the examiner.

Examiners scan your answer specifically for comparative discourse markers. Using these connectives guarantees your points are credited as comparisons rather than isolated observations:

Signalling Similarities (Agreement)

  • "Both Text A and Text B agree that..."
  • "Similarly, Text B also highlights..."
  • "In the same way, the author of Text B..."
  • "Likewise, both writers acknowledge..."

Signalling Differences (Contrast)

  • "Whereas Text A argues..., Text B claims..."
  • "In sharp contrast, Text B focuses on..."
  • "On the other hand, the writer of Text B..."
  • "Conversely, while Text A uses formal statistics..."
Level 1 Level 2 Pearson Edexcel City & Guilds NCFE

Core Skill 3: Comparing Content vs Comparing Methods (Level 1 vs Level 2)

Understand when to compare facts/opinions and when to compare language/presentational techniques.

Always read the exact wording of the comparison question:

  • "Compare the views / information in Text A and Text B" (L1 & L2): Focus on what the writers say — their opinions, costs, benefits, and factual claims, supported by short quotes from each text.
  • "Compare how the writers convey their views" (L2 Higher-Order Question): You must compare both perspective AND language/tone (e.g., "Text A conveys a reassuring corporate view using positive statistics ('94% satisfaction'), whereas Text B conveys bitter frustration using hyperbole ('an absolute staffing catastrophe').").
Level 1 Level 2 Pearson Edexcel City & Guilds NCFE

Pearson Edexcel Step-by-Step Worked Models

Benchmark Exam Model 1 2 Marks (Pearson Edexcel Reading Level 1)

Vocational Context: Workplace Catering Consultation

Read the two extracts:
Text A (Facilities Manager): 'Replacing our staffed hot canteen with smart vending machines will save £22,000 a year and provide 24-hour food access for night-shift workers.'
Text B (Warehouse Supervisor): 'While 24-hour access for night staff is welcome, vending machines only sell processed snacks rather than the hot, nutritious meals our manual workers need.'
(a) Identify one point of agreement between Text A and Text B. [1 Mark]
(b) Identify one point of disagreement about the food provided. [1 Mark]
Step
Description / Working
Criteria
Step 1 Identify shared agreement across both extracts
Both Text A and Text B agree that providing 24-hour food access for night-shift staff is a positive benefit.
Criteria: 1 Mark [A1 Similarity]
Step 2 Contrast their views on the food using a connective
Whereas Text A views smart vending machines as a cost-saving upgrade (£22,000 saved), Text B argues they only provide 'processed snacks' instead of the hot, nutritious meals workers need.
Criteria: 1 Mark [A1 Difference]
Final Accredited Response: (a) Agreement: Both agree that 24-hour food access for night-shift workers is beneficial. | (b) Disagreement: Text A promotes vending machines to save £22,000, whereas Text B criticises them for serving processed snacks instead of hot, nutritious meals.
Marking Scheme Guidance & Assessment Criteria
Even in a 2-mark Level 1 question, always mention both texts explicitly when explaining a disagreement.
Benchmark Exam Model 2 4 Marks (Pearson Edexcel Reading Level 2)

Vocational Context: Healthcare Shift Scheduling Analysis

Read the two extracts:
Text A (Healthcare Facility Circular): 'Transitioning to 12-hour nursing rosters has reduced clinical handover errors by 15% and guarantees seamless operational continuity for acute patients.'
Text B (Nursing Union Blog): 'Exhausted nurses are being pushed to breaking point! Working gruelling 12-hour marathons doubles medication errors after the tenth hour — how can exhausted staff keep patients safe?'
Compare the writers' views on 12-hour shifts and how they use language to convey their perspectives. [4 Marks]
Step
Description / Working
Criteria
Step 1 Compare central viewpoints on patient safety with quotes
Both texts focus on patient safety during 12-hour shifts; however, Text A supports the shifts by claiming they 'reduced clinical handover errors by 15%', whereas Text B opposes them, claiming they 'double medication errors after the tenth hour'.
Criteria: 2 Marks [A1 Content & Quote Comparison]
Step 2 Compare tone and language devices with quotes
Text A uses a formal, reassuring corporate tone with professional vocabulary ('seamless operational continuity'). In contrast, Text B uses an urgent, emotive tone featuring metaphors ('gruelling 12-hour marathons') and a rhetorical question ('how can exhausted staff keep patients safe?') to provoke concern.
Criteria: 2 Marks [A1 Language & Tone Comparison]
Final Accredited Response: Text A supports 12-hour shifts, citing a '15%' drop in handover errors in a formal, objective tone ('seamless operational continuity'). Conversely, Text B strongly opposes 12-hour shifts, warning that 'medication errors' double and using emotive language ('pushed to breaking point') and a rhetorical question ('how can exhausted staff keep patients safe?') to highlight staff burnout.
Marking Scheme Guidance & Assessment Criteria
Always mention BOTH texts in EVERY comparative paragraph. If you write only about Text A in paragraph 1 and Text B in paragraph 2 without cross-referencing, examiners cap your mark.

Critical Examiner Traps & Common Pitfalls

Examiner Pitfall: Writing Separate Summaries Without Direct Comparison
Writing a summary of Text A followed by an unrelated summary of Text B prevents you from demonstrating synthesis and caps your mark at 1 out of 4.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Use comparative sentence frames: 'While Text A argues [X], Text B contends that [Y].'
Examiner Pitfall: Comparing Unmatched Topics (Apples vs Oranges)
Saying 'Text A talks about parking costs whereas Text B talks about uniform rules' is not a valid comparison because the two points are not about the same sub-topic.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Always match the exact same theme across both texts (e.g. how both view costs, or how both view staff morale).

Interactive Exam Practice Questions

Exam Practice Question 1 3 Marks
Read the two extracts on town centre parking:
Text 1 (Council Press Release): 'Increasing high-street parking tariffs by £1.20 per hour will relieve severe daytime congestion and cut town centre exhaust emissions by 20%.'
Text 2 (Shopkeepers' Petition): 'Although town centre traffic is undeniably congested, this greedy £1.20 parking hike will drive loyal shoppers straight to out-of-town retail parks!'
Identify one similarity and two differences in how the texts present the parking issue.
Marking Scheme Criteria:
1. Similarity: Both Text 1 and Text 2 agree that town centre parking/traffic is heavily congested [1 Mark].
2. Difference 1 (Impact): Text 1 argues the £1.20 increase will cut emissions by 20%, whereas Text 2 warns it will drive shoppers away to out-of-town retail parks [1 Mark].
3. Difference 2 (Tone/Language): Text 1 uses formal, objective statistics ('20%'), whereas Text 2 uses emotive, critical language ('greedy', exclamation mark) [1 Mark].
Final Model Answer: Similarity: Both agree the town centre is congested. | Difference 1: Text 1 claims the £1.20 rise cuts emissions by 20%, whereas Text 2 claims it will drive shoppers to retail parks. | Difference 2: Text 1 uses a formal statistical tone, while Text 2 uses emotive language ('greedy').
Examiner Tip: Ensure you clearly label which text is which and use comparative connectives like 'whereas' and 'both'.
Exam Practice Question 2 3 Marks
Read the two extracts about open-plan offices:
Text A (Office Architect): 'Open-plan workspaces foster instant team collaboration, spark creative ideas, and reduce partitioning costs by 35%.'
Text B (Software Developer): 'Working in a noisy fishbowl of constant chatter destroys deep concentration and leaves neurodivergent staff exhausted by 3 p.m.'
Compare how the two writers view open-plan offices and identify one language technique used in Text B.
Marking Scheme Criteria:
1. Viewpoint Comparison: Text A views open-plan offices positively as collaborative and cost-effective ('reduce partitioning costs by 35%'), whereas Text B views them negatively as distracting and exhausting ('destroys deep concentration') [2 Marks].
2. Language Technique in Text B: Metaphor ('noisy fishbowl') or emotive verb ('destroys'), emphasizing lack of privacy and sensory overload [1 Mark].
Final Model Answer: Text A praises open-plan offices for boosting collaboration and cutting costs by 35%, whereas Text B criticises them for ruining concentration and exhausting staff. Text B uses the metaphor 'noisy fishbowl' to highlight the lack of privacy.
Examiner Tip: Quote short phrases from both extracts to anchor your comparison.
Exam Practice Question 3 4 Marks
Read these two extracts on AI customer service chatbots:
Text A (Tech Vendor): 'Our AI Assistant resolves 82% of routine billing queries in under 10 seconds, freeing human agents to handle complex cases.'
Text B (Consumer Watchdog): 'Frustrated customers are trapped in endless automated loops when trying to report urgent banking fraud. Why replace empathetic human advisors with robotic scripts?'
Compare how Text A and Text B present AI chatbots, referring to both content and language. [4 Marks]
Marking Scheme Criteria:
1. Content comparison: Text A presents AI chatbots as fast and helpful ('resolves 82% of routine billing queries in under 10 seconds'), whereas Text B presents them as obstructive during urgent emergencies ('trapped in endless automated loops') [2 Marks].
2. Language comparison: Text A relies on persuasive statistics ('82%', '10 seconds') and a professional tone, whereas Text B uses emotive imagery ('trapped in endless... loops') and a rhetorical question ('Why replace empathetic human advisors with robotic scripts?') [2 Marks].
Final Model Answer: Text A portrays AI chatbots as efficient support tools that free up staff ('resolves 82%... in under 10 seconds'), using factual statistics. In contrast, Text B portrays them as frustrating barriers ('trapped in endless automated loops'), using emotive language and a rhetorical question to defend human advisors.
Examiner Tip: Four-mark comparison questions require two well-developed comparisons supported by direct quotes from both texts.
