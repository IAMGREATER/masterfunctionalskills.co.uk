Three Non-Negotiable Exam Golden Rules

Golden Rule 1: Recognise Social Engineering Channels (Phishing, Smishing, Vishing)

Phishing = fraudulent email. Smishing = fake SMS texts (`Post Office £2.99 redelivery fee`). Vishing = voice scam phone calls pretending to be Police, HMRC, or IT Support.

✗ Assuming only weird messy emails can be phishing — modern spear-phishing looks clean!
✓ Spot panic urgency, mismatch domains, unusual payment requests across Email/SMS/Calls
Golden Rule 2: Always Hover Over Links & Inspect Full Sender Domain After '@'

Link text saying `https://www.clinic.org/login` can silently point to `http://evil-steal.ru` underneath! Hover mouse gently over buttons before clicking.

✗ Clicking big blue `Verify Account Now` buttons because the company logo matched
✓ Verify true hover destination URL + navigate manually via official bookmark
Golden Rule 3: Report Phishing Internally to IT / Forward Emails to `report@phishing.gov.uk`

Forward suspicious UK scam emails to the NCSC Suspicious Email Reporting Service (`report@phishing.gov.uk`) or scam texts to `7726`, and warn workplace IT quickly if clicked by mistake.

✗ Silently hiding a mistake if you accidentally typed a work password into a scam page
✓ Alert IT Security immediately so compromised passwords & sessions are revoked fast

Step-by-Step Teaching & Key Concepts

1
How Social Engineering Works

Social Engineering manipulates human emotions (fear, urgency, greed, or curiosity) rather than hacking hardware directly. Criminals know that tricking a human into clicking a link is often easier than breaking computer encryption.

Key variations include:

  • Phishing (Email): Fake emails appearing to come from banks, delivery couriers, or senior managers.
  • Smishing (SMS / Text Messages): Scam texts about missed parcel deliveries or banking blocks containing shortened links.
  • Vishing (Voice / Phone Calls): Scammers impersonating the police, Microsoft technical support, or your bank fraud team on the telephone.
2
Inspecting Sender Domains and Hyperlinks

Never trust the display name alone! Anyone can set their display name to 'Healthcare IT Support' or 'HM Revenue & Customs'.

  1. Check the actual email address: Look at the characters after the @ symbol. If it says support@hmrc-tax-gov-service99.net, it is fake. Official HMRC emails end in @hmrc.gov.uk.
  2. Hover to Reveal (URL Snooping): Hover your mouse cursor over any hyperlink without clicking. A small preview box will appear showing the real web address. If the text says 'www.bank.co.uk' but the hover link points to 'www.scam-login-portal.ru', it is fraudulent!
Hyperlink Hover Preview Simulation
Fake IT Support Notification

Your cloud storage is full. Click here to upgrade your storage

Real URL: http://phish-login-steal.xyz/account
3
Workplace Incident Reporting

If you receive a phishing email at work:

  • Do NOT click any links, do NOT open any attachments, and do NOT reply.
  • Click the Report Phishing button in Outlook, or forward the email as an attachment to your organisation's official IT security helpdesk.
  • Delete the message from your Deleted Items folder.

Accredited Worked Exam Models (2 Scenarios)

Study both realistic assessment scenarios below alongside official examiner mark allocations ([B1] knowledge, [M1] process, [A1] accuracy).

Worked Model 1 • Core StandardStep-by-Step Accredited Guide
Exam Scenario Prompt: A clinic worker receives an email that appears to come from 'Payroll Department' with the subject 'URGENT: Payroll Verification Required'. The email states that wages will be stopped unless the employee clicks a link and enters their bank login details. State two clues that indicate this is a social engineering attack, and describe the action the employee should take.
Step 1: Identify two phishing clues [B1]
1. Uses high psychological pressure / urgency (threatening to stop wages). 2. Requests private banking login credentials via an emailed link (legitimate payroll departments never ask for bank passwords).
Tutor Marking Rationale: Identifies psychological manipulation and credential harvesting.
Step 2: Describe correct workplace action [B1]
Do not click the link; report the email immediately to the internal IT security department and delete it.
Tutor Marking Rationale: Correctly follows organisational reporting and remediation protocol.
Tutor Abdul's Exam Golden Rule: In Level 1 exams, never say 'Reply to ask if it's real'. The accredited action is ALWAYS: 'Do not click links; report to IT and delete'.
Worked Model 2 • Applied Workplace Scenario Exam Scenario Model 2 • Handling 'Executive CEO Gift Card' Spear-Phishing
Exam Scenario Prompt 2: An accounts clerk gets an email displaying her Director's exact name, sent from `director.name.office@gmail.com`: 'I'm stuck in a board meeting — quickly buy 5 x £100 digital gift cards and email me the codes now, confidential!' Diagnose and respond safely.
Step 1: Spot Freemail Domain Spoof & Out-of-Channel Urgency [B1]
Although the display name matches the Director, `@gmail.com` is an external free webmail account (not the company domain), combined with classic artificial secrecy/rush ('confidential! now!').
Tutor Marking Rationale: Catches specific Business Email Compromise (BEC) / Spear-phishing cues.
Step 2: Independent Out-of-Band Verification & Reporting [B1][M1]
Do NOT reply to the email or purchase cards. Call/message the Director directly using their verified internal extension/Teams profile, then click 'Report Phishing' in Outlook.
Tutor Marking Rationale: Out-of-band confirmation defeats social engineering every time.
Tutor Abdul's Distinction Tip: Notice lookalike typo-squatting domains (`rnicrosoft.com` using `r+n`, `paypa1.com` using digit `1`, `nhs-portal-verify.co`) — read every letter between `//` and the first `/` slowly from right to left!

Critical Examiner Traps & Common Pitfalls

Trap 1: Believing 'HTTPS Padlock Guarantees the Website Owner Is Honest'
Even fake scam sites use free HTTPS padlocks nowadays! Padlock ONLY proves the pipe is encrypted, NOT that the scammer owns your bank!
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Check BOTH padlock AND exact authentic spelling of the domain name.
Trap 2: Opening Unexpected Password-Protected '.ZIP' Invoices
Scammers lock malware inside ZIPs with a password printed in the email body so antivirus filters can't peek inside before download.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Never unzip unexpected passworded email invoices from unverified contacts.
Trap 3: Replying 'STOP' or Challenging Scammers Directly
Engaging proves a human reads that inbox, triggering escalated vishing/targeted attacks.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Delete/Report directly using official reporting tools without replying.

Independent Practice Exam Task

Draft your full response in the auto-saving workspace below before checking Tutor Abdul's benchmark solution.

Onscreen Assessment Task:
A staff member accidentally clicked a link in a phishing email and entered their workplace network password before realising it was a scam. List the immediate three steps the employee must take to protect the organisation.
Your Working & Answer Scratchpad: Auto-saves in browser

Accredited Exam Benchmark Solution

Step 1: Change their network password immediately from a clean device. Step 2: Disconnect the computer from the network (unplug network cable / turn off Wi-Fi) to stop malware spreading. Step 3: Contact the workplace IT security team immediately to report the compromise so they can reset sessions and check account logs.
Official Marking Criteria:
  • [1 Mark]: Immediately changing the compromised password.
  • [1 Mark]: Reporting to IT security and isolating device from the network.
