Strong Passwords, Personal Privacy and Spotting Scams

Protect yourself and your workplace from online crime: create strong, unbreakable passwords, keep personal data private, and spot scam emails, phishing messages, and suspicious links.

Create strong, secure passwords combining letters, numbers, and symbols Identify sensitive personal information that must never be shared publicly Recognise the warning signs of scam emails, fake texts (smishing), and phishing links

Step-by-Step Teaching & Key Concepts

Core Knowledge
1
Creating Strong Passwords

A weak password is like leaving your front door unlocked. Cybercriminals use automated software that guesses thousands of common passwords every second.

  • Weak Passwords (BAD): 123456, password, admin, your pet's name, or your birthday.
  • Strong Password Rules: At least 8 to 12 characters long, containing a mixture of UPPERCASE letters, lowercase letters, numbers (0-9), and symbols (!@#$%).
  • Passphrase Method: Combine three or four random words: Purple!Tiger#Jumps9. Easy for you to remember, impossible for hackers to guess!
Golden Rule of Passwords: Never share your password with anyone! IT support, banks, and managers will NEVER ask you for your password.
2
Protecting Personal Data (Privacy)

Personal Data is any information that identifies you or someone else. Never post the following on public social media or unverified websites:

  • Your full date of birth, home address, or national insurance number.
  • Bank account numbers, PINs, or credit card security codes (CVV).
  • Workplace photos showing patient ID badges, hospital files, or computer screens.
3
Spotting Phishing and Scam Emails

Phishing is a trick used by criminals who pretend to be a trustworthy organisation (like HMRC, Royal Mail, your bank, or IT department) to steal your login details.

Look out for these four telltale warning signs:

  1. Sense of Extreme Urgency: 'Your account will be suspended in 24 hours unless you click here immediately!'
  2. Generic Greeting: 'Dear Customer' or 'Dear User' instead of your real name.
  3. Suspicious Sender Address: The display name says 'Royal Mail', but the actual address is delivery@random-scam-xyz123.com.
  4. Fake Web Links: Hover your mouse over the link without clicking — look where it really points!
Phishing Email Warning Breakdown
Suspicious Message Alert
From: UK Tax Office <refund@service-gov-tax-update88.net>
Subject: URGENT: You have an unclaimed tax refund of £485.20!

Dear Citizen,
Click the link below to enter your debit card and claim immediately:
http://claim-tax-now-update.info/login

Authentic Pearson Edexcel Exam Model

Accredited Model

Here is how this skill is tested in the real Pearson Edexcel onscreen digital assessment, broken down with official examiner mark codes ([B1] knowledge, [M1] process, [A1] accuracy).

Exam Scenario Prompt: A clinic worker receives an unexpected email stating: 'Your clinic email password will expire today! Click this link to confirm your password or your account will be deleted.' State two reasons why the worker should suspect this is a phishing scam.
Step 1: Identify first warning sign [B1]
It creates a false sense of urgency/panic (threatens account deletion today).
Tutor Marking Rationale: Urgent threatening language is a classic social engineering tactic.
Step 2: Identify second warning sign [B1]
It asks the user to click a link to reveal/enter their password.
Tutor Marking Rationale: Legitimate IT services never send links requesting current passwords.
Tutor Abdul's Exam Golden Rule: In Entry 3 exams, if an email asks for your password or card details via a link, it is ALWAYS a phishing scam. Never click the link!

Onscreen Assessment Task:
You receive a text message on your phone saying: 'Royal Mail: Your parcel has an unpaid fee of £1.45. Pay now at www.royalmail-fee-pay.xyz or your parcel will be returned.'

State two reasons why this is a scam, and describe what action you should take.

Pearson Edexcel Benchmark Solution

Reason 1: The web link is suspicious and fake (ends in .xyz rather than official royalmail.com). Reason 2: It uses urgent language demanding money for a parcel you may not even have ordered. Action: Do not click the link, do not enter any card details, report and delete the message.
Official Marking Criteria:
  • [1 Mark]: Identifying fake website address / urgent demand for money.
  • [1 Mark]: Stating not to click the link and deleting/reporting the message.
