Sentence Basics — Capital Letters & Full Stops

Subi
A  The Concept in Everyday Use: Shift Handover Log & Daily Operational Notes
★B  Complete Sentence Architecture: Subject + Finite Verb + Complete Thought
A complete grammatical sentence must express a single finished thought and contain two essential pillars:
Capital Letter → [Subject: Who/What] + [Finite Verb: Action/State] + [Complete Sense] → Full Stop (.)
  • Sentence Fragment: Missing a finite verb or subject (e.g. "Because the conveyor stopped." — this is an incomplete dependent clause).
  • Run-on Sentence: Two independent sentences fused together with no terminal punctuation (e.g. "The conveyor stopped the engineer arrived.").
  • Full Stop Rule: Conclude every declarative sentence with a solid full stop (.). The subsequent sentence must open with a crisp capital letter.
✗ Common Mistake
the morning shift finished at eight all safety gates was locked secure. Because the team followed protocol.
✓ Correct Sentence
The morning shift finished at eight. All safety gates were locked securely because the team followed protocol.
Why: Capitalised the sentence starters ('The', 'All'), split the run-on with a full stop, and joined the dangling fragment into a complete causal sentence.

Question Marks vs Exclamation Marks in Customer Service

Priya
A  The Concept in Everyday Use: Customer Helpdesk & Client Correspondence
★B  Direct Inquiry vs Urgent Exclamation Grammar
Punctuation governs customer tone and transactional clarity:
[Direct Question] → Inversion (Auxiliary + Subject) → Question Mark (?)
[Safety Hazard / Urgent Warning] → High Alarm → Exclamation Mark (!) (Maximum ONE)
  • Direct Question: Requires an answer or action from the reader (e.g. "Would you prefer a refund or an exchange?"). Conclude with a question mark.
  • Indirect Statement: Do not place a question mark on indirect reported questions (e.g. "The customer asked if the parcel had arrived." — end with a full stop).
  • Exclamation Control: Reserved exclusively for urgent hazard warnings (e.g. "Caution! Wet floor."). Never use multiple marks (!! or !?!?) in workplace writing.
✗ Common Mistake
Could you please confirm your invoice number. Hurry up and reply immediately!!!
✓ Correct Sentence
Could you please confirm your invoice number? Please reply at your earliest convenience.
Why: Replaced the careless full stop with a required question mark and eliminated triple exclamation marks to maintain polite professional composure.

Capital Letters for Proper Nouns, Acronyms & Titles (MINTS)

Marcus
A  The Concept in Everyday Use: Conference Registration & Corporate Identity
★B  The MINTS Framework for Flawless Capitalisation
Remember the five essential categories that demand capital letters:
M Months & Days • I The Pronoun 'I' • N Names & Places • T Titles & Headings • S Start of Sentences
  • Months & Days: Always capitalise Monday, Friday, January, September. Do not capitalise general seasons (spring, autumn) unless part of a proper title.

On Wednesday, I accompanied Dr Green to Manchester General Hospital to inspect the new PPE store.
Why: Applied capitals to day ('Wednesday'), pronoun ('I'), title ('Dr Green'), hospital proper name ('Manchester General Hospital'), and acronym ('PPE').

Joining Sentences with Coordinating Conjunctions (And, But, Or, So)

Sarah
A  The Concept in Everyday Use: Incident Log & Risk Assessment Updates
★B  The FANBOYS Toolkit: Coordinating Equal Clauses
Coordinating conjunctions connect two independent, grammatically equal clauses:
[Independent Clause 1] + , [and / but / or / so] + [Independent Clause 2]
  • AND: Adds similar information (e.g. "The fire alarm sounded, and all staff evacuated immediately.").
  • BUT: Signals an unexpected contrast (e.g. "The engineer arrived on time, but the replacement valve was missing.").
  • OR: Offers choices or alternatives (e.g. "You can submit the paper timesheet, or you can log your hours online.").
  • SO: Shows a direct logical result (e.g. "The access road was flooded, so deliveries were redirected.").
✗ Common Mistake
The forklift battery died. The operator plugged it in. The backup charger was broken.
✓ Correct Sentence
The forklift battery died, so the operator plugged it in, but the backup charger was broken.
Why: Combined choppy repetitive fragments using 'so' (logical outcome) and 'but' (contrast), improving flow and cohesion.

Joining Sentences with Subordinating Conjunctions (Because, Although, When)

Sam
A  The Concept in Everyday Use: Staff Performance Appraisal & Action Planning
★B  Complex Sentence Hierarchy: Main Clause + Subordinate Clause
A complex sentence contains an independent main clause and a dependent subordinate clause:
[Main Clause] + [because / although / when / if] + [Subordinate Clause]
[Subordinating Conjunction] + [Subordinate Clause] + , + [Main Clause]
  • BECAUSE (Reason): "Hannah exceeded her sales target because she phoned prospective clients daily."
  • ALTHOUGH (Concession): "Although the software crashed twice, James submitted the payroll report on schedule."
  • WHEN / IF (Condition/Time): "When the customer arrives, please escort them to Meeting Room 3."
  • Comma Rule: When the subordinate clause comes FIRST, place a comma after it. When it comes second, no comma is required.
✗ Common Mistake
Because Priya completed her training course early. She was promoted to team leader.
✓ Correct Sentence
Because Priya completed her training course early, she was promoted to team leader.
Why: Removed the incorrect full stop that isolated the subordinate clause into a fragment, adding a required comma before the main clause.

Commas in Everyday Lists of Items & Actions

Subi
A  The Concept in Everyday Use: Catering Stock Orders & Kitchen Inventory
★B  Listing Mechanics: Items, Adjectives & Actions
Commas separate three or more items, modifiers, or verb phrases in a series:
[Item 1] , [Item 2] , [Item 3] and [Item 4]
  • Serial Commas: Use commas between every item in a list of three or more nouns (e.g. "Please order milk, butter, cheddar cheese and double cream.").
  • Actions in Series: Separate consecutive verb phrases (e.g. "The technician checked the fluid level, replaced the gasket, cleaned the housing and tested the pump.").
  • Two Items Only: Never insert a comma when joining only two items (e.g. "Order bread and butter." NOT "Order bread, and butter.").
  • The 'And' Rule: In standard Level 1 UK writing, do not place a comma immediately before or after 'and' unless required to prevent ambiguity.
✗ Common Mistake
The pantry order requires flour sugar eggs, baking powder, and, vanilla essence.
✓ Correct Sentence
The pantry order requires flour, sugar, eggs, baking powder and vanilla essence.
Why: Inserted missing commas between the first three items and deleted unnecessary commas around 'and'.

Apostrophes for Contractions (Don't, Can't, Wouldn't)

Marcus
A  The Concept in Everyday Use: Staff Shift Cover & Immediate Operational Messaging
★B  Contraction Mechanics: The Apostrophe Replaces Missing Letters
An apostrophe of contraction marks exactly where letters have been omitted when two words merge:
do not → don't (omits 'o') • cannot → can't (omits 'no') • would not → wouldn't (omits 'o')
  • Exact Letter Replacement: The apostrophe sits inside the word precisely where the missing letter used to be (e.g. does not → doesn't; NOT does'nt).
  • Top Contractions: isn't, aren't, hasn't, haven't, didn't, won't, it's (= it is).
  • Exam Awareness: In formal letters and incident reports, examiners prefer full uncontracted forms (do not, will not), but in informal emails and chat logs, contractions must be formed flawlessly.
✗ Common Mistake
We could'nt open the safe because the supervisor has'nt arrived and they dont have the key.
✓ Correct Sentence
We couldn't open the safe because the supervisor hasn't arrived and they don't have the key.
Why: Corrected misplaced apostrophes in 'couldn't' and 'hasn't' (between 'n' and 't'), and added the missing apostrophe to 'don't'.

Apostrophes for Singular Possession (The customer's order)

Sarah
A  The Concept in Everyday Use: Customer Returns Desk & Lost Property Ledger
★B  Singular Ownership Architecture: Singular Noun + 's
To show that ONE person, animal, or entity owns something, add an apostrophe followed by 's':
[Singular Owner: Noun] + 's + [Item Possessed]
  • Singular Ownership: the customer's order (one customer), the doctor's signature (one doctor), the company's policy (one company).
  • Names Ending in -s: For singular names ending in -s, add 's in modern functional English (e.g. James's locker, Charles's coat).
  • No Ownership = No Apostrophe: Simple plurals do not take an apostrophe (e.g. "Five customers waited" NOT "Five customer's waited").
  • Its vs It's: Its (no apostrophe) shows possession (e.g. "The cat licked its paw; the printer lost its power"). It's ALWAYS means it is.
✗ Common Mistake
We found the drivers phone in the managers office near the staff lockers.
✓ Correct Sentence
We found the driver's phone in the manager's office near the staff lockers.
Why: Added apostrophes to show singular ownership: one driver owned the phone ('driver's'), and one manager occupied the office ('manager's').

Plural Nouns — Regular (-s, -es) and Irregular (children, men, feet)

Sam
A  The Concept in Everyday Use: Workplace Facilities & Welfare Provision Audit
★B  Pluralisation Rules: Regular Suffixes vs Irregular Roots
Plural nouns represent more than one person, place, or thing:
Regular: Noun + -s • Hissing Sounds (-ch, -sh, -s, -x, -z): Noun + -es • Irregular: Root Vowel Shift
  • Standard Rule (-s): desk → desks, tool → tools, computer → computers.
  • The -es Rule: When a singular noun ends in -s, -ss, -sh, -ch, or -x, add -es to create a pronounceable syllable (e.g. box → boxes, branch → branches, bus → buses).
  • Top Irregular Plurals: Never add -s to nouns that change their root vowels or forms: man → men, woman → women, child → children, foot → feet, tooth → teeth, person → people.
  • No Apostrophe for Plurals: Never use an apostrophe to form a simple plural (e.g. "apple's for sale" is WRONG; write "apples").
✗ Common Mistake
All fire boxs must be checked by the two security mans and their childs.
✓ Correct Sentence
All fire boxes must be checked by the two security men and their children.
Why: Added -es to 'box' ('boxes') and replaced incorrect pluralisations 'mans' and 'childs' with proper irregular forms 'men' and 'children'.

-FUL = Full of (Always ONE 'l'!) • -LESS = Without (Always TWO 's's!) • -LY = Adverb (How something is done) • -MENT = State or result of action
  • The -FUL Rule: The suffix -ful has ONLY ONE 'L': careful, helpful, successful, grateful, stressful. Never spell it with -full!
  • The -LESS Rule: careless, spotless, endless, painless.
  • The -LY Rule: Turns adjectives into adverbs: quick → quickly, polite → politely, careful → carefully (notice: careful + ly = carefully with two 'l's!).
  • The -MENT Rule: Creates nouns from verbs: develop → development, manage → management, equip → equipment. Keep the silent 'e' in most cases!
✗ Common Mistake
Marcus is a carefull worker who completes every assignement quickley and shows great managment.
✓ Correct Sentence
Marcus is a careful worker who completes every assignment quickly and shows great management.
Why: Corrected 'carefull' to single 'l' (-ful), fixed spelling of 'assignment' and 'quickly', and restored silent 'e' in 'management'.

Functional Skills SPaG Glossary

Capital Letter
Used at the start of every sentence, for the word 'I', people's names, places, days, and months.
Full Stop
Punctuation mark (.) used to mark the end of a complete statement.
Question Mark
Punctuation mark (?) used at the end of a direct question.
Exclamation Mark
Punctuation mark (!) used to show strong feeling, surprise, or urgency.
Comma in Lists
Used to separate items in a list (e.g. 'paper, pens, and pencils').
Conjunction
A linking word used to join two sentences (e.g. and, but, so, because).
Past Tense
Verb forms used to describe things that happened yesterday or in the past (e.g. walked, saw).
Present Tense
Verb forms used for things happening now or regularly (e.g. walks, sees).
Subject-Verb Agreement
Matching singular subjects with singular verbs (e.g. 'He works' vs 'They work').
Homophone
Words that sound the same but have different spellings and meanings (e.g. there, their, they're).
