Capital Letters for 'I', Personal Names & Titles

Subi
A  Look & Learn:
Official Identity & Workplace Registration
★B  Core Syntax Architecture: 4 Rules for Capital Letters
[1] Pronoun 'I' + [2] Formal Titles (Mr, Mrs, Ms, Dr) + [3] First Names + [4] Surnames
  • The Personal Pronoun 'I': Always capitalised when standing alone (e.g. "Today I completed my induction." — never a small 'i').
  • Formal Titles: Capitalise abbreviations for people's titles (Mr Green, Mrs Patel, Ms Davies, Dr Ahmed).
  • First Names & Surnames: Every person's proper name begins with an uppercase letter (James Wilson, Fatima Begum).
✗ Not Quite Right
yesterday i met mr hassan and we signed the training contract.
✓ Good Sentence
Yesterday, I met Mr Hassan and we signed the training contract.
Tip: 'Yesterday' starts the sentence; 'I' is the standalone pronoun; 'Mr' is a title; 'Hassan' is a surname. All require capital letters.

Full Stops — Ending a Complete Thought

Priya
A  Look & Learn:
Marking Clear Sentence Boundaries in Shift Reports
★B  Core Syntax Architecture: The Complete Thought Cycle
[Capital Letter] + Subject (Who / What) + Predicate Verb (Action) + Full Stop [ . ]
  • Complete Thought Requirement: A sentence must make sense on its own. It tells the reader who did what.
  • The Sentence Boundary: Place the full stop firmly at the end of the thought. Do NOT put a comma where a full stop is needed.
  • The Reset: Every full stop must be followed immediately by a space and a capital letter for the next sentence.
✗ Not Quite Right
the security guard checked the perimeter fence he found no damage.
✓ Good Sentence
The security guard checked the perimeter fence. He found no damage.
Tip: These are two separate complete actions. Leaving out the full stop creates a confusing run-on sentence.

Question Marks — Asking for Help & Information

Sam
A  Look & Learn:
Asking for Workplace Clarification & Client Assistance
★B  Core Syntax Architecture: The Question Inversion Pattern
Question Starter + Subject + Action Verb + Question Mark [ ? ]

  • Modal & Auxiliary Starters: Can, Could, Would, Do, Does, Is, Are.

could you send me the updated stock catalogue.

Could you send me the updated stock catalogue?
Tip: 'Could you send...' is a direct request asking someone to take action and reply. It requires a question mark at the end.

Simple Sentences — Subject, Verb, Object (SVO)

Sarah
A  Look & Learn:
Building Strong, Clear Sentences in the Workplace
★B  Core Syntax Architecture: The SVO Sentence Engine
[1] Subject (Who/What) + [2] Verb (Action/State) + [3] Object / Detail
  • The Subject: The person, machine, or thing performing the action (e.g. The technician, The alarm).
  • The Verb: The action word or state of being (e.g. repaired, tested, is). Every sentence MUST have a verb.
  • The Object / Completer: The person or thing receiving the action (e.g. the motor, the safety valve).
✗ Not Quite Right
the new delivery boxes in the corridor outside bay 2.
✓ Good Sentence
The warehouse assistant stacked the delivery boxes outside bay 2.
Tip: The pitfall is a fragment because it has no verb. Adding the subject 'assistant' and the verb 'stacked' creates a complete, grammatical sentence.

Joining Sentences with 'And' (Adding information)

Marcus
A  Look & Learn:
Connecting Related Workplace Tasks Smoothly
★B  Core Syntax Architecture: The Addition Bridge Formula
[Idea 1: Complete Sentence] + and + [Idea 2: Complete Sentence or Verb]
  • Adding Information: 'And' links two ideas of equal importance (e.g. "I sorted the mail and I delivered the parcels.").
  • Shared Subjects: If both actions are done by the same person, you can connect the verbs directly (e.g. "Emma swept the floor and emptied the bins.").
  • The Run-on Warning: Do not use 'and' more than once in the same sentence. Never write: 'I did this and I did that and I went here and...'
✗ Not Quite Right
I checked the delivery and I counted the boxes and I signed the slip and I left.
✓ Good Sentence
I checked the delivery and I counted the boxes. Then, I signed the slip.
Tip: Overusing 'and' creates an endless, exhausting run-on sentence. Use 'and' once to join two ideas, then use a full stop.

Joining Sentences with 'But' (Contrasting information)

Priya
A  Look & Learn:
Reporting Exceptions & Contrasting Information
★B  Core Syntax Architecture: The Contrast See-Saw
[Idea 1: Normal / Expected Fact] + , but + [Idea 2: Unexpected Contrast / Problem]
  • Contrasting Ideas: 'But' connects two clauses that show an opposite or surprising outcome (e.g. "The alarm sounded, but there was no fire.").

✓ Good Sentence
We called the technician three times, but he did not answer.
Tip: Not answering is an unexpected, contrasting problem. The connective 'but' is required to show the contrast.

Cause & Effect with 'So'

Subi
A  Look & Learn:
Reporting Actions Taken as a Direct Result
★B  Core Syntax Architecture: The Result Consequence Chain
[Problem / Situation] + , so + [Action Taken / Consequence]
  • Showing the Result: 'So' connects a problem or event to the resulting action (e.g. "The printer ran out of paper, so I refilled the tray.").
  • Because vs So: 'Because' introduces the REASON; 'So' introduces the RESULT (e.g. "The floor was wet, so I mopped it." vs "I mopped the floor because it was wet.").

✗ Not Quite Right
The floor was greasy because I put down warning cones.
✓ Good Sentence
The floor was greasy, so I put down warning cones.
Tip: Putting down cones is the result of the greasy floor, not the cause of it. The connective 'so' is required.

Present Tense — Daily Workplace Routines (Regular Verbs)

Marcus
A  Look & Learn:
Documenting Daily Routines & Standard Operating Procedures
★B  Core Syntax Architecture: The Daily Routine Framework
I / You / We / They + Base Verb (No Ending) + Routine Time Expression
  • Habitual Everyday Actions: Use base verbs for routines: I check, we clean, they inspect, you sign.
  • Time Expressions: Frequently paired with: every day, each morning, on Mondays, regularly, always.
  • The Past Tense Trap: Never add '-ed' when describing ongoing daily routines (e.g. NOT 'Every morning I checked the stock' — write 'I check').
✗ Not Quite Right
Every morning, I checked the temperature gauges and logged the readings.
✓ Good Sentence

Diagnostic Assessment Worked Solutions (Questions 1–10)

Q1B: The personal pronoun 'I' and formal names ('Mr Ahmed') must always be capitalised.
Q2C: Days ('Monday'), towns ('Leeds'), and months ('March') all require capital letters.
Q3A: Brands ('Tesco Express'), streets ('High Street'), cities ('Manchester'), postcodes ('M4 5BD').
Q4B: Two complete thoughts require a full stop and capital letter: '...kitchen. He locked...'
Q5A: 'Could you show me...' is a direct enquiry and requires a question mark.
Q6B: Safety warnings take a single exclamation mark to indicate urgency: 'Danger!'
Q7B: 'The warehouse assistant' (Subject) + 'stacked' (Verb) + 'the delivery boxes' (Object).
Q8A: 'And' connects the two actions smoothly without repeated words or extra commas.
Q9A: 'But' shows the contrast between what was ordered and what arrived.
Q10A: 'Because' links the main clause to the reason without creating a sentence fragment.
Q11B: 'So' introduces the action taken as a direct result of the wet floor.
Q12A: Commas separate list items; 'and' links the final pair without extra commas.
Q13A: Everyday routine actions with 'I' take the base present tense ('check' and 'log').
Q14B: Singular third-person subject ('Marcus') requires an '-s' ending: 'locks' and 'sets'.
Q15A: Regular past actions add '-ed' to the base verbs: 'inspected' and 'called'.
Q16B: Short vowel + consonant doubles the final letter: 'stop' -> 'stopped', 'plan' -> 'planned'.
Q17B: Plural subject takes 'were' ('managers were'); past tense of see is 'saw'.
Q18A: Standard irregular past forms: 'came' (not comed), 'took' (not taked), 'gave' (not gived).
Q19A: Singular subject ('apprentice') agrees with singular verbs 'has' and 'is'.
Q20B: Plural subjects ('doors', 'keys') agree with plural verbs 'were'.
Q21B: Nouns ending in -x ('box') and -sh ('dish') take '-es': 'boxes' and 'dishes'.
Q22A: Consonant + y changes to '-ies': 'factory' -> 'factories', 'city' -> 'cities'.
Q23A: 'Two' (number 2), 'their' (belonging to them), 'there' (in that location).
Q24A: Full capitalisation of 'I' and 'Leeds', with proper full stops between sentences.
