Subi — Lead Assessor & Specialist Maths Tutor Examiner Guidance

Welcome to the Rounding & Directional Estimation blueprint. In non-calculator Section A papers, examiners specifically require you to round every number to 1 significant figure BEFORE calculating. Remember the golden rule: rounding before calculating gives you full method marks [M1], whereas calculating the exact answer first and rounding at the end scores zero!

Specification & Learning Outcomes

Candidates must demonstrate mastery of the following regulated Functional Skills Mathematics outcomes:

  • L1.6 – Round numbers to a given power of 10, specified decimal places, or significant figures.
  • L1.7 – Estimate answers to multi-step calculations by rounding each value to 1 significant figure.

Three Non-Negotiable Golden Rules for Rounding & Estimating

Golden Rule 1: The '5 or More Round UP' Decider Rule

Identify your target rounding column, then look strictly at the single digit immediately to its right (the Decider Digit):

DECIDER IS 0, 1, 2, 3, or 4 → STAY THE SAME
43.28 → 43

The decider digit is 2 (less than 5), so the target unit digit 3 remains unchanged.

DECIDER IS 5, 6, 7, 8, or 9 → ROUND UP BY +1
47.86 → 48 ✓

The decider digit is 8 (≥ 5), so the target unit digit rounds up from 7 to 8.

Golden Rule 2: Round BEFORE Calculating in 1 s.f. Estimation Checks

In Functional Skills exams, whenever you see "Use estimation to check your answer", you MUST round each number to 1 significant figure before calculating. Doing long calculation first and rounding at the end scores 0 marks!

CALCULATING FIRST (FORFEITS [M1])
194 × £4.85 = £940.90 → £1,000

The mark scheme strictly forbids exact calculations for estimation questions. You receive 0/2 marks.

ROUNDING FIRST (FULL MARKS [M1][A1])
200 × £5 = £1,000 ✓

Round 194 → 200 and £4.85 → £5 first. Instant mental maths earns full accredited marks!

Golden Rule 3: Maintain Positional Placeholders for Whole Numbers

When rounding large whole numbers to significant figures, never drop the trailing placeholder zeros. Placeholders maintain the order of magnitude:

Rounding 14,682 to 2 Significant Figures:
14,682 → 15,000 (NEVER 15!)

The 3 zeros are critical placeholders for hundreds, tens, and units.

Rounding Decimals (No Extra Zeros):
4.382 to 1 dp → 4.4 (NOT 4.400)

Do not add trailing zeros to decimals unless formatting currency (£).

Core Skill 1: Decimal Places vs Significant Figures Comparison Directory Core Convention

In Level 1 exams, candidates frequently confuse Decimal Places (dp) with Significant Figures (sf). Understand the golden difference:

Decimal Places (dp)

Start counting digits only after the decimal point, moving left to right. Ignore all numbers to the left of the decimal point.

Significant Figures (sf)

Start counting from the first non-zero digit on the far left (whether before or after the decimal point). Leading zeros NEVER count!

Leading Zeros Trap: In 0.00715, the zeros before the 7 do NOT count as significant figures. They simply tell you where the decimal point lives. The first significant figure is 7.
Core Skill 2: Estimating Complex Calculations by Rounding to 1 Significant Figure Exam Check Benchmark

In Non-Calculator exams, complex multi-step fractions can be estimated in seconds by replacing each messy value with its 1 significant figure round:

Estimate: 58.7 × 19.4 0.48
Stage 1: Round to 1 s.f.
58.7 → 60  |  19.4 → 20  |  0.48 → 0.5
[M1] Rounding Mark
Stage 2: Solve Numerator
Numerator = 60 × 20 = 1,200
6 × 2 = 12, add two zeros
Stage 3: Divide by 0.5
1,200 ÷ 0.5 = 2,400 ✓
[A1] ESTIMATE = 2,400
Non-Calculator Division Secret: Dividing any number by 0.5 is exactly identical to multiplying it by 2 (doubling it)!
1,200 ÷ 0.5 = 1,200 × 2 = 2,400.
Core Skill 3: Identifying Overestimates vs Underestimates Explanation Mark [B1]

Pearson Edexcel and City & Guilds frequently attach an explanation mark [B1]: "Is your estimate an overestimate or an underestimate? Give a reason." Here is the complete decision logic:

Rounding Direction Mathematical Impact Accredited Status Exam Reason Template
Both Values Rounded UP
e.g. 194 → 200, £4.85 → £5		 Estimated product is LARGER than actual value (£1,000 > £940.90) OVERESTIMATE "Overestimate because both values were rounded up."
Both Values Rounded DOWN
e.g. 312 → 300, £2.15 → £2		 Estimated product is SMALLER than actual value (£600 < £670.80) UNDERESTIMATE "Underestimate because both values were rounded down."
One UP, One DOWN
e.g. 194 → 200 (UP), £5.20 → £5 (DOWN)		 The rounding errors partially cancel each other out COMPENSATING "Close estimate as one rounded up and one rounded down."

Pearson Edexcel Step-by-Step Worked Models

Benchmark Exam Model 1 3 Marks (Pearson Edexcel Level 1/2 Non-Calculator)

Vocational Context: Community Care Food Services Procurement

A healthcare facility catering manager orders 194 cartons of meal trays at £4.85 each.
(a) Show an estimate of the total expenditure by rounding each value to 1 significant figure.
(b) State whether your estimate is an underestimate or overestimate, giving a clear mathematical reason.
Step 1 Round Each Value to 1 Significant Figure [M1] Both Values Rounded to 1 s.f.
1 S.F. ROUNDING
194 → 200
£4.85 → £5.00
For 194, the first digit is 1 and decider is 9 (≥ 5), rounding up to 200.
For £4.85, the first digit is 4 and decider is 8 (≥ 5), rounding up to £5.00.
Step 2 Compute the Estimated Total Expenditure [A1] Correct Estimated Product
ESTIMATED PRODUCT
200 × £5 = £1,000
Multiply the rounded quantities mentally: 2 × 5 = 10, then append the two zeros = £1,000.00.
Step 3 Determine Overestimate vs Underestimate with Stated Reason [B1] Accurate Justification
DIRECTION REASONING
194 < 200 (UP)
4.85 < 5.00 (UP)
→ OVERESTIMATE
Because both the quantity (194 → 200) and the unit price (£4.85 → £5) were rounded UP, the resulting estimate (£1,000) is definitely larger than the true cost (£940.90).
FINAL ACCREDITED ANSWER: (a) Estimate = £1,000 | (b) Overestimate, because both 194 and £4.85 were rounded up.
Sonya's Key Rule: To justify whether an estimate is an underestimate or overestimate, state the direction of BOTH rounded values! Since 194 was rounded UP to 200 and £4.85 was rounded UP to £5, the product is guaranteed to be an overestimate.

Critical Examiner Traps & Common Pitfalls

Pitfall 1: Calculating the Exact Product First in Estimation Questions

When an exam question instructs "Use estimation to check your answer", performing exact multi-digit arithmetic (e.g. 194 × £4.85 = £940.90) and then rounding to £1,000 scores 0/2 marks!

✗ Candidate Mistake: 194 × 4.85 = 940.90 → 1,000 (0 MARKS) ✓ Accredited Rule: 200 × 5 = 1,000 (FULL MARKS)
How to Avoid: Round each number to 1 s.f. on your first line of working before calculating anything.
Pitfall 2: Dropping Trailing Placeholder Zeros on Whole Numbers

When rounding 14,682 to 2 significant figures, candidates often write 15. 15 is fifteen; 15,000 is fifteen thousand! Dropping placeholder zeros changes the order of magnitude.

✗ Candidate Mistake: 14,682 → 15 (LOST MARK) ✓ Accredited Rule: 14,682 → 15,000 (3 PLACEHOLDER ZEROS)
How to Avoid: Check that the rounded number has the exact same number of digits before the decimal point as the original number.
Pitfall 3: Counting Leading Zeros in Decimals as Significant Figures

In 0.0048, candidates often incorrectly count the leading zeros and assume it has 4 or 5 significant figures.

✗ Candidate Mistake: 0.0048 has 4 s.f. ✓ Accredited Rule: 0.0048 has exactly 2 s.f. (4 and 8)
How to Avoid: Start counting significant figures only when you reach the first digit from 1 to 9.

Interactive Exam Practice Questions

Exam Practice Question 1 2 Marks (Non-Calculator)
Use 1 significant figure approximation to estimate the value of:
38.4 × 9.75 0.48
Marking Scheme Step-Down Breakdown:
1. Round Each Value to 1 s.f. [M1]: 38.4 → 40, 9.75 → 10, and 0.48 → 0.5
2. Compute Numerator: 40 × 10 = 400
3. Divide by 0.5 [A1]: 400 ÷ 0.5 = 400 × 2 = 800
Final Accredited Answer: 800
Examiner Tip: Dividing by 0.5 is equivalent to doubling the numerator (400 × 2 = 800).
Exam Practice Question 2 2 Marks (Accredited Standards)
Round the number 14,682.739 to:
(a) 2 decimal places
(b) 2 significant figures
Marking Scheme Step-Down Breakdown:
(a) 2 Decimal Places [B1]: Look at 3rd digit after dot (9 ≥ 5). Rounds .73 up to .74 → 14,682.74
(b) 2 Significant Figures [B1]: First 2 digits are 14. Decider is 6 (≥ 5), rounding 14 up to 15. Keep placeholder zeros → 15,000
Final Accredited Answers: (a) 14,682.74 | (b) 15,000
Examiner Tip: Never write 15 for part (b)! You must include the three placeholder zeros for hundreds, tens, and units.
Exam Practice Question 3 3 Marks (Level 1 Multi-Step)
A charity event sells 392 tickets at £19.80 each. Fixed room hire and entertainment expenses total £1,895.00.
Estimate the net fundraising profit by rounding each value to 1 significant figure.
Marking Scheme Step-Down Breakdown:
1. Round to 1 s.f. [M1]: 392 → 400 tickets, £19.80 → £20, £1,895 → £2,000
2. Estimated Revenue [M1]: 400 × £20 = £8,000
3. Deduct Estimated Expenses [A1]: £8,000 − £2,000 = £6,000.00
Final Accredited Answer: Net Profit Estimate = £6,000.00
Examiner Tip: Show all 3 rounded values (400, £20, £2,000) on your very first line.
