Specification & Learning Outcomes

Candidates must demonstrate mastery of the following regulated Functional Skills English outcomes:

  • L2.14 – Recognise bias and determine whether statements represent verifiable facts, balanced evaluation, or one-sided subjective persuasion.
  • L1.20 / L2.23 – Use language, register, and style suited to audience, purpose, and medium across formal and informal contexts.

Golden Rules for Formality, Register & Detecting Bias

Golden Rule 1: Bias Stems from One-Sided Evidence & Loaded Words

An author is biased when they present only one side of an issue, omit legitimate counter-arguments, have a commercial/personal vested interest, or use emotionally loaded vocabulary ('disastrous', 'miraculous') to manipulate the reader.

Golden Rule / Technique: Bias Checklist: Vested Interest • One-Sided Facts • Loaded Adjectives
Golden Rule 2: Match Formality to Professional Hierarchy

Formal register avoids slang, exclamation marks, and contractions ('don't' -> 'do not'). It uses precise vocational terminology, objective phrasing, and standard salutations ('Dear Ms Patel / Yours sincerely').

Golden Rule / Technique: Formal: No contractions • Objective tone • Standard salutations

Core Skill 1: Detecting Overt vs Covert Bias in Functional Texts

Three forensic reading tests to expose author bias and vested interest in Level 2 exams.

In Level 2 Reading exams, you are frequently asked whether a text is balanced or biased, and to provide evidence from the passage. Apply these three tests:

  1. Check the Author's Vested Interest (Byline / Bio): Who wrote or funded the text? If the Sales Director of a private scooter firm writes an article praising electric scooters and attacking cycle lanes, they have a direct commercial motive.
  2. Inspect Evaluative Adjectives (Loaded Language): Compare neutral reporting ("The council revised the bin collection schedule") with biased reporting ("Reckless councillors forced through a chaotic bin scheme").
  3. Check for Omission of Counter-Arguments: A balanced article includes concessions ("While critics point out the £5m cost, supporters highlight..."). A biased text ignores all drawbacks completely.

Core Skill 2: The Register Calibration Table: Informal vs Formal English

How to identify register in Reading and upgrade informal phrasing in Writing.

Linguistic Feature Informal / Conversational Register Formal / Professional Register
Contractions & Slang "We can't put up with dodgy Wi-Fi any longer!" "Staff cannot operate effectively with unreliable network connectivity."
Verb Choice Phrasal verbs: look into, sort out, let down, put off. Single Latinate verbs: investigate, resolve, disappoint, postpone.
Typical Text Types Community forum posts, peer-to-peer emails, friendly blogs. Statutory reports, job application letters, official complaint responses.

Core Skill 3: How Writers Blend Informality with Professional Purpose

Why some workplace texts deliberately use a semi-formal, conversational register.

Not all workplace texts are strictly formal. In Level 2 exams, you will often see semi-formal articles or staff intranet guides that combine accurate facts with conversational hooks (e.g., "Feeling frazzled by Friday? Our new Employee Assistance Helpline offers confidential clinical counselling 24/7.").

Why do writers do this? Using an approachable, conversational opening builds rapport and prevents readers from feeling intimidated, while the formal factual details build trust and credibility.

Pearson Edexcel Step-by-Step Worked Models

Benchmark Exam Model 1 2 Marks (Pearson Edexcel Reading & Writing)

Vocational Context: Customer Service Complaint Handling

Read this draft sentence from a trainee manager's letter to a client:
'Sorry our driver messed up your Tuesday delivery — we'll sort out a replacement parcel ASAP.'
(a) Identify two features that make this sentence too informal for a client letter. [1 Mark]
(b) Rewrite the sentence in an appropriate formal register. [1 Mark]
Step
Description / Working
Criteria
Step 1 Identify informal colloquialisms and contractions
Uses colloquial phrasal verbs ('messed up', 'sort out'), a contraction ('we'll'), and an informal conversational abbreviation ('ASAP').
Criteria: 1 Mark [A1 Register Analysis]
Step 2 Convert into formal professional Standard English
'Please accept our sincere apologies for the error with your delivery on Tuesday; we will arrange a replacement consignment immediately.'
Criteria: 1 Mark [A1 Formal Rewrite]
Final Accredited Response: (a) Informal features: slang/phrasal verbs ('messed up', 'sort out') and contraction ('we'll'). | (b) Formal rewrite: 'We sincerely apologise for the error regarding your Tuesday delivery and will dispatch a replacement order immediately.'
Marking Scheme Guidance & Assessment Criteria
Replacing phrasal verbs ('messed up' -> 'error', 'sort out' -> 'arrange') is the fastest way to elevate register in formal letters and reports.
Benchmark Exam Model 2 3 Marks (Pearson Edexcel Reading Level 2)

Vocational Context: Healthcare Procurement Appraisal

Read the promotional extract written by the Sales Director of MedTech Solutions:
'Our revolutionary CareFlow tablet system is unquestionably the most advanced clinical app ever built. Outdated paper charts are a catastrophic waste of taxpayer funds, and any care home that fails to install CareFlow immediately is putting residents in grave danger.'
Explain why this extract is biased, supporting your answer with two quotations and commenting on the writer's position. [3 Marks]
Step
Description / Working
Criteria
Step 1 Quote and explain hyperbolic superlative language
'Unquestionably the most advanced clinical app ever built' — uses an unverified commercial superlative as if it were an indisputable fact.
Criteria: 1 Mark [A1 Quote & Explanation 1]
Step 2 Quote and explain loaded fear-mongering language
'Catastrophic waste' and 'putting residents in grave danger' — uses emotionally manipulative language to guilt care home managers into buying the product.
Criteria: 1 Mark [A1 Quote & Explanation 2]
Step 3 Evaluate the author's vested commercial interest
The writer is the Sales Director of MedTech Solutions, meaning they have a direct financial motive to praise CareFlow and omit any setup costs or software limitations.
Criteria: 1 Mark [A1 Vested Interest]
Final Accredited Response: The text is biased because it is written by the Sales Director of MedTech Solutions, who has a financial motive to sell the product. It uses exaggerated superlatives ('unquestionably the most advanced') and emotionally loaded fear tactics ('putting residents in grave danger') without providing independent clinical evidence.
Marking Scheme Guidance & Assessment Criteria
In Functional Skills Reading, linking the author's job title/vested motive to their choice of loaded words secures maximum marks.

Critical Examiner Traps & Common Pitfalls

Examiner Pitfall: Assuming Any Strong Argument is Automatically Biased
An article is NOT automatically biased just because the author reaches a clear conclusion, provided they weigh up evidence from both sides fairly first.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Look for unfair suppression of counter-arguments, vested financial interest, or emotionally loaded insults before declaring a text biased.
Examiner Pitfall: Using Contractions in Formal Letters and Reports
Writing 'don't', 'can't', or 'it's' in a formal letter to a council or employer signals inconsistent register control.
How to Avoid This Pitfall: Always expand contractions to 'do not', 'cannot', and 'it is' in formal tasks.

Interactive Exam Practice Questions

Exam Practice Question 1 2 Marks
A trade union newsletter states: 'Greedy depot bosses are deliberately slashing shift allowances to line their own pockets.'
Explain how the writer's language indicates bias in this sentence, quoting specific words.
Marking Scheme Criteria:
1. Pejorative loaded language: The author uses emotive, insulting words ('Greedy', 'slashing') to attack management [1 Mark].
2. Unproven accusation/one-sided view: Claims bosses act 'to line their own pockets' without presenting management's operational reasons or financial evidence [1 Mark].
Final Model Answer: Uses loaded, insulting vocabulary ('Greedy', 'slashing') and makes an unproven personal accusation ('line their own pockets') without giving management's perspective.
Examiner Tip: Always quote the exact loaded words inside quotation marks when answering bias questions.
Exam Practice Question 2 2 Marks
Read this extract from a staff health guide:
'Ditch the lift and take the stairs! You'll burn calories, boost your mood, and dodge that mid-afternoon slump.'
Describe the register and tone of this extract and give one language feature that creates it.
Marking Scheme Criteria:
1. Register and tone: Informal, lively, friendly, and encouraging [1 Mark].
2. Language feature: Colloquial imperatives ('Ditch the lift', 'dodge'), contraction ('You'll'), or direct address ('your mood') [1 Mark].
Final Model Answer: Register/Tone: Informal, friendly, and upbeat. | Feature: Colloquial verbs ('Ditch', 'dodge') or contraction ('You'll').
Examiner Tip: Pair a clear tone adjective (informal/upbeat) with a quoted word from the sentence.
Exam Practice Question 3 3 Marks
Read this extract from a consumer technology review:
'While the Apex Laptop boasts a crisp 4K display and a 14-hour battery life, its £1,600 price tag and lack of USB-A ports make it difficult to recommend for budget-conscious students.'
Is this extract balanced or biased? Explain your answer with evidence from the text. [3 Marks]
Marking Scheme Criteria:
1. Judgment: The extract is balanced / objective [1 Mark].
2. Evidence of positives: It acknowledges specific strengths ('crisp 4K display and a 14-hour battery life') [1 Mark].
3. Evidence of negatives: It weighs those strengths fairly against drawbacks ('£1,600 price tag and lack of USB-A ports') to give a reasoned conclusion [1 Mark].
Final Model Answer: Balanced: The reviewer weighs up positive features ('crisp 4K display', '14-hour battery life') alongside negative limitations ('£1,600 price tag', 'lack of USB-A ports') using measured, non-emotive language.
Examiner Tip: Look out for exam questions where the text is actually balanced! Mentioning both the pros and cons quoted in the text proves why it is unbiased.
